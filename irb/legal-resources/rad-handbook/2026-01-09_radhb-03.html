3. New issues

3.1. What is a new issue?

The Courts have found the following factors relevant in identifying a new issue:

  • Issues that are legally and factually distinct from the grounds of appeal raised by the parties and cannot reasonably be said to stem from the issues raised on appeal.Note 1
  • Issues where the RPD makes no determination.Note 2

Not all these factors need to be satisfied for the issue to be identified as a new issue.

Despite the above factors, it is acknowledged that “there is a fine (and sometimes blurred) line” between situations where the RAD raises and deals with a new issue and those where it simply refers to an additional piece of evidence on the record to support an already existing conclusion of the RPD on a factual assessment or on an existing credibility issue.Note 3

As the cases discussed later in this chapter demonstrate, the Federal Court’s application of these factors to the facts and issues before them has resulted in conflicting case law. In similar cases, the Court has found the RAD’s findings to be a new issue, while in other cases has found the opposite.

3.2. The Refugee Appeal Division's jurisdiction to raise new issues

It is accepted in the jurisprudence that the RAD has the jurisdiction to raise an issue on its own initiative, which was not addressed by the RPD in its reasons nor raised by a party on appeal.Note 4 The Federal Court has also found that deciding an appeal on a basis not addressed by the RPD raises an issue of procedural fairness, rather than one of jurisdiction.Note 5

3.2.1. Does the Refugee Appeal Division have jurisdiction to raise a new issue if it does not find an error in the Refugee Protection Division's decision?

There is conflicting jurisprudence about whether the RAD must find an error in the RPD decision before deciding the appeal on another basis.

In Angwah, the Federal Court took the view that the RAD’s failure to make a finding that the RPD erred before making its own determination on a new ground was not in keeping with the Court’s decision in Huruglica, and therefore, unreasonable.Note 6

However, in Okechukwu the Court stated that it was not necessary for the RAD to find the RPD erred before the RAD can consider an alternate ground on which to uphold a decision dismissing the claim. In the Court’s view, this would be contrary to the legislator’s intent and the Federal Court of Appeal’s decision in Huruglica that matters heard by the RAD not be referred back to the RPD for redetermination unless it is clear that: the RPD had erred in law or in fact or mixed fact and law; and the RAD cannot make a decision without hearing evidence presented to the RPD.Note 7

In this case, the RAD reasoned that it could decide on an alternate ground because paragraphs 111(1)(a) and 111(1)(b) of the Immigration and Refugee Protection Act (IRPA) gave it the power to confirm or substitute the “determination” of the RPD, and as such, it is not bound by the reasoning in the RPD’s decision. Furthermore, the RAD concluded, the restrictions on returning claims to the RPD for redetermination under subsection 111(2) suggests that Parliament’s intent was to have the RAD finalize refugee protection claims where it can do so fairly, including by confirming a determination on alternative grounds.Note 8

3.3. The requirement to give notice of a new issue to the person and to the Minister

If the RAD pursues a new issue, procedural fairness requires that the party or parties affected be given notice and an opportunity to make submissions.Note 9

The Federal Court has also held that the Minister has a broad and continuing interest in appeals; and therefore, has a right to procedural fairness even in appeals in which the Minister is not yet a party.Note 10 This right includes a right to notice that a new issue is being considered by the RAD and an opportunity to make submissions in relation to the new issue.Note 11

3.3.1. What is the scope and content of the notice of a new issue?

3.3.1.1. General principles

The appropriate procedure to give notice of a new issue will vary depending on the context and the circumstances of a given case and provided the following guidelines:

  • Notification of the new issue may occur before, during, or after the hearing. The issue should be raised as soon as is practically possible after the issue crystallizes to avoid any undue delay in the proceedings.
  • Notification should not contain too much detail or indicate that the court has already formed an opinion; however, it must contain enough information to allow the parties to respond to the new issue.
  • Ultimately, the adequate content of notice will have to be determined on a case-by-case basis. It will depend on several factors, including the complexity of the issue and the obviousness of the issue on the face of the record.Note 12

The Federal Court has commented that how such notice should be given in refugee appeals is for the RAD to determine, but the suggestions in R v. Mian listed above may be of assistance.Note 13

3.3.1.2. Content of notice in the Refugee Appeal Division context

The jurisprudence has not described in great length what should be included or how much detail should be provided in the RAD’s notice of a new issue. The Federal Court has said that although the scope of the duty will vary, at minimum, the parties should have some opportunity to respond to the RAD’s concerns.Note 14 This includes the opportunity to provide submissions and additional evidence that is responsive to the RAD's notice of a new issue.Note 15 In several cases, parties have challenged the level of detail provided by the RAD in its notice of a new issue.

In Azenabor, a case involving new issues of credibility, the Federal Court stated that the level of detail required in the notice will depend on the facts, but it does not require the RAD to put each of its credibility concerns to the person. In that case, it was sufficient notice for the RAD to raise concerns about the credibility of the person’s police report in comparison to sample police reports in the National Documentation Package (NDP), without enumerating all its credibility concerns with their report.Note 16

In Berhani, the person argued that the RAD failed to give sufficient notice of the case to meet on the issue of prospective risk in the IFA location. In the notice, the RAD sought submissions on the continuing motivation of the agent of persecution in the context of an omission from the person’s narrative. The Federal Court found there was no breach of procedural fairness because the notice about the omission spoke to the continuing motivation of the agent of persecution, which engaged the first branch of the IFA test.Note 17

However, in Collins the Federal Court found that the RAD breached procedural fairness because the RAD’s general notice stating that “credibility” was at issue, was not sufficient to afford the person an opportunity to respond to a particular inconsistency being raised for the first time in the RAD’s reasons. The Court stated that although the person did have the RPD record and transcript, as well as the Minister’s Intervention letter, without at least some basic description of the RAD’s areas of concern with respect to credibility, the person was required to try to identify what these credibility concerns might be and to make anticipatory submissions, rather than providing submissions in response to specific credibility concerns.Note 18 In contrast, the Court in Collins also found that a different credibility finding made by the RAD did not breach procedural fairness. This is because the RPD raised the inconsistency with the person at the hearing, the person was aware of this concern and could, and did, address it in their response to the RAD. Thus, the Court did not consider this other credibility concern to require a more specific description in the notice of new issues.Note 19

In Nasr, the Federal Court allowed the judicial review of a RAD decision due to the lack of detail in the RAD’s notice of a new issue. In its notice, the RAD simply stated it found “the dates of the documents and the alleged issuing authorities to be problematic” and noted “that police stations with detention facilities in Egypt are listed in the Global Detention Project report”. The RAD then provided a link to the report in the notice. In its decision, the RAD found the person’s documents to be inauthentic because the name of the police stations on the person’s documents did not match the names of the police stations for the city in question in the Global Detention Project report. The Court held that the RAD’s vague reference to concerns with “the alleged issuing authorities” and a link to the report meant that the person would have been unaware of what the RAD’s real concern was with the documents.Note 20

3.3.2. Remedies for failing to give notice of a new issue

3.3.2.1. The general rule and exception to the general rule

Generally, if the Court finds that the process was unfair, it will send the matter back to the RAD to be decided again.Note 21 However, the Court may disregard this general rule if the Court finds that the unfairness would not have impacted the outcome.Note 22

This exception to the general rule has been recognized in several cases where it was argued that the RAD breached procedural fairness by raising a new issue without notice.Note 23 However, in most of the cases that discuss the exception to the general rule, the Federal Court held that the RAD did not raise a new issue.Note 24

3.4. No breach of procedural fairness for the Refugee Appeal Division to raise a new issue and not address it in its reasons

Where the new issue is not determinative of the appeal, the Federal Court has held that it is not a breach of procedural fairness for the RAD to raise a new issue, but not address it in its reasons or refer to the parties’ submissions and supporting evidence on the new issue.Note 25

3.5. Case law on new issues at the Refugee Appeal Division

The most common circumstances when it is argued the RAD raised a new issue is when the RAD makes additional credibility findings. However, arguments that the RAD raised a new issue also appear in the consideration of issues of identity, IFA, state protection, nexus, well-founded fear, exclusion, and other matters considered by the RAD in its decision-making. What follows are examples of the circumstances where the Court has found the RAD raised a new issue as well as those where the Court found the RAD did not raise a new issue.Note 26

The following cases demonstrate that the Federal Court’s application of the above-mentioned factors, to the facts and issues before them, has resulted in conflicting case law. In similar cases, the Court has found the RAD’s findings to be a new issue, while in other cases has found the opposite.

3.5.1. Cases where the Federal Court found the Refugee Appeal Division raised a new issue

The Federal Court has found that the RAD raised a new issue in making findings on many different elements of a refugee claim, including:

  • Credibility
    • Additional reasoning or substantive findingsNote 27
    • Witness evidenceNote 28
    • General finding of lack of credibilityNote 29
    • Assessment of documents, including authenticityNote 30
  • Additional findings and the National Documentation Package (NDP)Note 31
  • IdentityNote 32
  • Internal flight alternative (IFA)Note 33
  • State protectionNote 34
  • NexusNote 35
  • Well-founded fear of persecution
    • Objectively well-founded fear of persecutionNote 36
    • Prospective riskNote 37
  • Sur place claimsNote 38
  • ExclusionNote 39
  • Admissibility of new evidenceNote 40

3.5.2. Cases where the Federal Court found the Refugee Appeal Division did not raise a new issue

The Federal Court has found the RAD did not raise new issues in making findings on many different elements of a refugee claim, including:

  • Credibility
    • Additional reasoning or substantive findingsNote 41
    • Assessment of documents, including authenticityNote 42
    • The person's response to the RAD's notice of a new credibility issueNote 43
  • Sufficiency of the evidenceNote 44
  • Internal Flight Alternative (IFA)Note 45

The RAD made an additional finding, noting that there was no evidence on appeal that the persons were still in therapy or that services were not available or accessible. The Court rejected the person’s argument that the RAD raised a new issue and found the RAD was dealing with sufficiency of evidence relating to the reasonableness of the IFA. In the Federal Court’s view, the insufficiency of the evidence pointed out by the RAD was not a new question. Rather, the onus was on the persons to put forward evidence and arguments supporting their position that the proposed IFA is unreasonable in view of their mental health. The RAD reasonably found that they had not done so.

Note 46

In Tan v. Canada (Minister of Citizenship and Immigration), 2016 FC 876, paragraph 49 (CanLII), the RPD accepted the person’s claim for refugee protection. The Minister appealed the RPD’s decision to the RAD and the RAD allowed the appeal and found the person was not a Convention refugee because there was adequate state protection in their country of nationality. On judicial review, the person argued that the RAD’s factual conclusions relied upon in support of its decision on the issue of state protection were not put to them and were new issues. The Federal Court held that the RAD’s findings did not raise a new issue and were related to the issue of state protection that was already raised in the RPD decision. The Court stated that the RAD simply came to a different conclusion than the RPD based on its independent assessment of the evidence, which included referring to documentary evidence in the RPD record.

Note 47

In Cardenas v. Canada (Minister of Citizenship and Immigration), 2017 FC 1194, paragraphs 14–15 (CanLII), the RPD rejected the claim on the grounds of credibility without deciding whether there was a nexus. On appeal, the RAD gave notice of the new issue of IFA and confirmed the RPD’s decision on this basis. In confirming the dismissal of the claim based on IFA, the RAD found there was no nexus. On judicial review, the persons argued the RAD’s finding that there was no nexus was a new issue and they should have been given an opportunity to make submissions on this issue. The Federal Court rejected the argument, finding that every claim for protection raises both the possibility of protection as a refugee on Convention grounds, and the possibility of surrogate protection under section 97. The issue of which, if either provision, applies to a person is always an issue before the RPD and the RAD.

Note 48

In Iqbal v. Canada (Minister of Citizenship and Immigration), 2020 FC 170, paragraphs 35—38 (CanLII), the person was a Pakistan citizen, and they feared their abusive spouse, who was a Bangladesh citizen. The RPD found the person had a viable IFA in Pakistan. The RAD found the RPD erred in the way it assessed the viability of an IFA in Pakistan, stating that to determine that a viable IFA exists, one must first determine that there is a well-founded fear of persecution in the country where the IFA is located. As there was no evidence of the person’s fear in Pakistan (only in Bangladesh), the RAD found that an IFA assessment was not applicable to the facts of this case. Instead, the RAD upheld the RPD’s decision based on the RAD’s own finding that the person had not articulated a well-founded fear of persecution in Pakistan based on the person’s admission that their abusive spouse could not harm them in Pakistan, their spouse’s lack of connection to Pakistan, and the lack of communication between the person and their spouse. On judicial review, the person argued the RAD raised a new issue by deciding the appeal based on their well-founded fear in Pakistan. The Federal Court dismissed the judicial review and found it was unclear whether the RAD raised a new issue, as the RAD simply reiterated the RPD’s reasoning regarding the risk of persecution in Pakistan, which relates to the first prong of the two-prong IFA test. Furthermore, the person did not challenge the RPD’s first prong analysis on appeal.

Note 49

In Henry v. Canada (Minister of Citizenship and Immigration), 2020 FC 1187, paragraphs 3, 6—9 (CanLII), the person claimed refugee protection due to a fear of persecution by reason of their conversion to Christianity. The RPD rejected the claim concluding that the person was not credible and that they did not establish they were Christian. On appeal, the RAD found that the RPD made several errors in its credibility assessment but confirmed that the person lacked credibility and did not meet their burden of establishing they subjectively feared persecution and that their fear was well-founded in an objective sense. On judicial review, the person argued the RAD ought to have given them an opportunity to respond to the RAD’s two determinative findings that there was no subjective fear and objective fear of persecution, which were not addressed by the RPD. With respect to the RAD’s finding that the person did not meet their burden of establishing they subjectively feared persecution, the Federal Court found this was not procedurally unfair. The Court noted the RAD relied on the record before it, notably the narrative and the RPD’s decision. Furthermore, the person was aware that on appeal, the determinative issue was their credibility and, in the alternative, their claim for refugee protection—that is, the existence of a subjective fear of persecution.

See also, Caleb v. Canada (Minister of Citizenship and Immigration), 2018 FC 384, paragraphs 20—24 (CanLII), where the RAD’s subjective fear findings were in response to the issue raised in the appeal regarding the RPD’s subjective fear findings and were based upon the RPD’s mistaken belief that the person’s children had accompanied them to the U.K. and Canada.

Note 50

In Baez De La Cruz v. Canada (Minister of Citizenship and Immigration), 2021 FC 457, paragraphs 6—12 (CanLII), the RPD rejected the claim on credibility. Moreover, the RPD found that no one had returned to the person’s mother’s home or other family members’ homes to try to find him after 2010, and the person had not established that he was currently being sought by the agent of persecution and their associates. On appeal, the RAD agreed with the RPD’s credibility findings, but also determined that even if it believed the person, it would still conclude that they did not establish a prospective risk. On judicial review, the person argued that the RAD raised a new issue of prospective risk since the sole issue before the RPD was credibility and they did not address the issue of prospective risk in their written submissions to the RAD. The Court disagreed, finding this is not a new issue because the existence of a prospective risk is always central to the right to protection under section 97 and it was clear from the RPD’s decision, read in light of the whole decision, that the RPD also had in mind the absence of a prospective risk and that it ruled on this issue, if not explicitly, then at least implicitly.

In Musthaffa v. Canada (Minister of Citizenship and Immigration), 2022 FC 59, paragraphs 31—32 (CanLII), the RPD rejected the claim based on a lack of credibility and lack of subjective fear. On appeal, the RAD rejected the appeal because there was no forward-looking risk. The Federal Court found that the RAD did not breach procedural fairness by deciding on a new issue of forward-looking risk because the forward-looking nature of the risk allegedly faced by the person was an inherent or implicit component of the RPD’s and the RAD’s analyses. The Court noted that the person’s express position on the appeal was that they faced a “personal risk” in returning to their country of nationality and they invited the RAD to look at all the evidence before the RPD and declare him to be a person in need of protection.

In Etienne v. Canada (Minister of Citizenship and Immigration), 2019 FC 1461, paragraphs 7, 12—15 (CanLII), the RPD rejected the claim for lack of credibility. The RAD did not consider the person’s credibility and dismissed the appeal for different reasons, that there was no risk to the person should they return to Haiti. The RAD found it could decide the matter based on the evidence because even if the person’s narrative was accepted as true, it was not enough to establish, on a balance of probabilities, that the person faced a risk if they were to return to Haiti. On judicial review, the person argued the RAD based its decision on a new set of arguments and breached procedural fairness by not giving them an opportunity to respond. The Federal Court rejected the person’s argument and found no notice was required because the person’s own argument to the RAD was that the RPD “committed an unreasonable error in law in finding that there was insufficient evidence with which to establish, on a balance of probabilities, that the [person] risks being persecuted”. By raising the argument themselves, the RAD was permitted to assess whether the person established a risk upon return to Haiti.

Note 51

In Lauture v. Canada (Minister of Citizenship and Immigration), 2023 FC 1121, paragraphs 20—27 (CanLII), the RPD excluded the person under Article 1E, after conducting an analysis of the risk he faced in his country of residence, Mexico. The RAD confirmed the exclusion, rejecting the person’s argument that the RPD should have given him notice that the objective basis of his fear in regard to Mexico would be a determinative issue. The RAD reasoned that risk is part of the analysis in Article 1E exclusion, and that the person had notice of Article 1E exclusion, so no notice of risk was required. On judicial review, the person argued both the RPD and the RAD breached procedural fairness. The Federal Court agreed with the RAD and found that there was no breach of procedural fairness. The Court found that the analysis of risk in the country of residence was part of the determinative issue of Article 1E exclusion, which implicitly included an analysis of subjective fear and objective basis in the country of residence.

In Gondal v. Canada (Minister of Citizenship and Immigration), 2023 FC 1226, paragraphs 10—20 (CanLII),the person argued on judicial review that the RAD breached procedural fairness by failing to give notice that it was considering the issue of Article 1E exclusion. The Federal Court found the Minister’s intervention at the RAD was notice of the issue of Article 1E and accordingly there was no breach of procedural fairness.

Note 52

In Uddin v. Canada (Minister of Citizenship and Immigration), 2022 FC 117, paragraphs 14 and 16 (CanLII), the Federal Court rejected the person’s argument that the RAD should have held an oral hearing or, alternatively, provided them with notice of the credibility concerns it had of the new evidence submitted on appeal before finding the new evidence was inadmissible because it lacked credibility. The Court stated the credibility assessment of the new evidence is a prerequisite to admitting the new evidence, and therefore, “[m]aking this assessment, which is required in every case, does not trigger a duty to give notice.” In fact, “[e]ven where the new evidence is tendered to overcome the RPD’s negative conclusions regarding the [person’s] overall credibility, the RAD does not raise a ‘new issue’ merely by finding the new evidence not credible.”

In Marquez Obando v. Canada (Minister of Citizenship and Immigration), 2022 FC 441, paragraphs 15, 26—28 (CanLII), the Court further noted that the RAD has the discretion to ask for written submissions on new evidence should it have questions on it, but it was not obliged to give the person another occasion, over and above RAD Rule 3(3)(g)(iii), to answer questions on the document’s credibility.
See also, Rehman v. Canada (Minister of Citizenship and Immigration), 2022 FC 783, paragraphs 27, 41 and 45 (CanLII), Metskhvarishvili v. Canada (Minister of Citizenship and Immigration), 2022 FC 1350, paragraphs 40—41 (CanLII), and Tuncdemir v. Canada (Minister of Citizenship and Immigration), 2016 FC 993, paragraph 43 (CanLII).

Note 53

In Zhao v. Canada (Minister of Citizenship and Immigration), 2019 FC 1593, paragraphs 27—31 (CanLII), the persons argued on judicial review that the RAD’s consideration of the change in country conditions in China constituted a new issue. The Federal Court disagreed and found it was not a new issue. The Court noted that the persons raised the two-child policy in their letter to the RAD and the RAD previously sought submissions from them on the two-child policy and the new information in the NDP. The Court also disagreed it was a new issue because their claim was based on their fear of forcible sterilization or abortion through the enforcement of China’s family planning policies, it had always been a central matter to their claim, which the RAD had a duty to consider. As a matter of procedural fairness, the RAD simply had a duty to disclose the most recent NDP and to give them an opportunity to respond and make submissions on this matter. This is exactly what the RAD did in this case.

Note 54

In Feng v. Canada (Minister of Citizenship and Immigration), 2019 FC 18 (CanLII), the Federal Court held that it was not a new issue for the RAD to rely on a new Jurisprudential Guide (JG) in its decision, even though the JG was released after the perfection of the appeal. The Court explained that the issues raised and addressed in the JG were not new or unknown to the person, as the person’s ability to exit China while being sought by the PSB was addressed by the RPD and an issue on appeal. Furthermore, the Court found the RAD did not act on its own initiative in considering the JG as it was implementing the IRB’s stated policy of addressing jurisprudential guidance in furthering the objective of promoting consistency in the treatment of similar cases before the IRB.

With respect to jurisprudence generally, see Yeshi v. Canada (Minister of Citizenship and Immigration), 2016 FC 1153, paragraphs 39—43 (CanLII). In Yeshi, the Federal Court found it was not a new issue for the RAD to refer to a recently decided Federal Court case (Tashi), which was issued after the perfection of the person’s appeal, when considering the application of the Williams test and whether the person had access to Indian citizenship. The Court held that it was not a new issue. This is because the issue of whether access to Indian citizenship was within their control was the same issue before the RPD, the person argued that the line of jurisprudence in Tretsetsang should not be followed and Tashi reflected one line of jurisprudence on the central issue, all of which the person was aware. However, the Federal Court stated at paragraph 43 that it may have been a good practice to bring the Tashi decision to the attention of the person and to invite submissions, considering the evolving jurisprudence.
