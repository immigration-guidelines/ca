8. Common Natural Justice Issues at the Refugee Appeal Division

This Chapter explores common procedural fairness and natural justice issues involving the RAD.

8.1. General principles

Subsection 162(2) of the IRPA provides that each Division of the Board should “proceed as informally and quickly as the circumstances and considerations of fairness and natural justice permit”. Natural justice comprises the rules that an administrative tribunal is required to observe to ensure fair decision-making. The two basic components of natural justice are 1) the right to be heardNote 1 and 2) the right to know the case to meet.Note 2 The concepts of natural justice and procedural fairness overlap.Note 3 The duty of procedural fairness is flexible and variable and depends on an appreciation of the context within which the decision is being made.Note 4 The independence of the Board, its adjudicative procedure and functions, and the fact that its refugee decisions affect the Charter rights of claimants, mean that the RPD and the RAD are held to a high standard of procedural fairness.Note 5

8.2. Refugee Appeal Division review of Refugee Protection Division fairness

Where the RAD finds that the RPD has breached procedural fairness, it must determine the appropriate remedy.Note 6 As a general rule, a breach of procedural fairness will render a decision void and the matter will be remitted for reconsideration.Note 7 The RAD reasons must show that statutory criteria are met to send a matter back to the RPD for redetermination.Note 8

In some cases where a remedy is required, the RAD may do what the RPD should have done and then make the determination.Note 9 The RAD has considerable error correction powers on appeal from the RPD.Note 10 For example, the RAD may disclose evidenceNote 11 or admit new evidence to cure possible breaches of procedural fairness.Note 12 In certain situations, the RAD may be required to provide a notice to the parties and provide them with an opportunity to make further submissions before remedying a breach of procedural fairness.Note 13

The RAD may not need to give a remedy where the merits of the claim are such that the outcome is legally inevitable or when the procedural error is purely technical and does not result in substantial wrong.Note 14

8.3. Right to be heard

Rooted in the right to be heard is the principle that individuals affected by a decision should have the opportunity to present their case fully and fairly.Note 15

8.3.1. Opportunity to state the case

The Federal Court has stated that the right to be heard is a cardinal principle of natural justice that should not be set aside based on a mere technicality.Note 16 It is an error to take an overly rigid approach to the application of procedural rules. Whether the Federal Court considers the Board’s approach to have been overly rigid is highly fact dependent. The Federal Court has found a breach of natural justice where the RPD refused to consider submissions that arrived somewhat late but before the decision was made.Note 17 The Court has also found a breach of natural justice where the RAD failed to consider a request to admit new evidence,Note 18 or failed to consider an intervention made by the Minister.Note 19

In contrast, the Federal Court has found that there is no breach of natural justice in denying a request at the time of the hearing to call a witness. The Federal Court held that since the claimant did not comply with the Rule to identify a witness prior to the hearing, the denial of a late request did not amount to a breach of procedural fairness.Note 20

For more information about the right to be heard, please see the sections on Adequacy of Interpretation (8.5) and Recordings and Transcripts (8.6) in this Chapter.

8.3.2. Right to know the case to be met

The Court has found that the RPD violated the claimant’s right to know the case to be met in the following situations:

  • where the RPD makes a negative credibility finding without first informing the claimant about its concern.Note 21
  • where the claimant has not made submissions on an issue, including credibility, because the RPD directly or indirectly indicates that no such submissions are required, and where the RPD makes its ruling based on that issue.Note 22

However, the duty of fairness does not require that the person be confronted with information that they themselves supplied.Note 23

Where, on appeal, the RAD decides to examine a new issue for the first time, notice of the new issue must be provided to the person and to the Minister, even if the Minister is not a party to the appeal.Note 24

In Miller, the Federal Court held that the Minister is entitled to receive notice of material changes to the person’s case, as they occur, even if the Minister is not a party to the proceeding.Note 25 Providing the Minister with notice of submissions or evidence filed after the perfection of the appeal provides the Minister with the opportunity to make an informed decision as to whether to intervene in the appeal.Note 26

For a detailed examination of the case law on new issues, please refer to Chapter 3: New Issues.

8.3.3. The one who hears must decide

One of the most fundamental rules in administrative law is the principle that only the member who heard the case, including reviewing the evidence and representations of the parties, may take part in the decision.Note 27  

The rule does not apply where legislation expressly or by necessary implication ousts its application.Note 28

The rule does not preclude all discussions with colleagues or the use of administrative and proofreading assistance as long as, in every case, the ultimate decision is that of the member.Note 29 For example, in Krasilov, the Court found that the person’s right to procedural fairness was not breached because the RAD member had their decision reviewed and edited by an assistant. The Court concluded that the emails between the member and the assistant “describe nothing more than the typical administrative assistance provided by support staff to decision makers”. The email exchange contained no indication that the member decided the matter on information unknown to the person, or that the member was substantially influenced by the assistant.

8.4. Right to representation

The right to representation is not absolute but the right to a fair hearing is.Note 30 A fair hearing requires that the person knows the case to meet and has a full and fair chance to respondNote 31. In the context of self-represented persons, decision-makers must ensure that they understand the nature of the proceedings and direct their attention to important points of procedureNote 32.

Where a self-represented claimant is prepared, agrees to go ahead, the tribunal explains the process, and the claimant appears to understand the proceedings, there is no breach of natural justice in proceeding without counsel.Note 33 Where despite the explanatory efforts and the goodwill of the tribunal, there is a failure of understanding on the part of the self-represented litigant, the Court has held that the unfairness must be remedied by holding a new hearing.Note 34

8.4.1. Allegations of counsel incompetence

The incompetence of counsel in the context of a refugee hearing is ground for review of the tribunal's decision on the basis of a breach of natural justice.Note 35 Allegations of counsel incompetence may form part of the grounds for appeal before the RAD. They may also be made in support of an application to re-open an appeal before the RAD or to re-instate a withdrawn appealNote 36 (also as a basis for the submission of new evidence to the RAD). This section of the chapter contains a summary of the jurisprudence, practices, rules and procedures that guide the RAD in deciding issues of counsel incompetence as they arise in proceedings before it.

8.4.1.1. Test for counsel incompetence

The Supreme Court of Canada set out a two-part test for establishing inadequate representation or counsel incompetence in R v. G.D.B.Note 37 This legal test applies to the RAD in determining allegations of counsel incompetence before it.

In order to demonstrate a breach of natural justice in a proceeding before the RAD, the person must demonstrate that

  • counsel’s acts or omissions constituted incompetence (performance component) and
  • that a miscarriage of justice resulted (prejudice component).Note 38

In cases where it is apparent that no prejudice has occurred, there is no need for the RAD to consider the performance component of the test.Note 39

The person is required to provide former counsel with a reasonable opportunity to respond.Note 40 This step is explored more fully under section 8.4.1.3.

8.4.1.2. Burden of proof

The person bears the burden of proof. The party making the allegation of incompetence must show substantial prejudice to the individual and that prejudice must flow from the actions or inaction of the incompetent counsel. It must be shown that there is a reasonable probability that, but for the counsel’s unprofessional errors, the result of the proceeding would be different.Note 41 This test does not require certainty. A reasonable probability is one that is sufficient to undermine the confidence in the outcome and lies somewhere between a mere possibility and a likelihood.Note 42

Incompetence will only result in procedural unfairness in extraordinary circumstances.Note 43 There is a strong presumption that counsel’s conduct falls within the wide range of reasonable professional assistance.Note 44 Moreover, incompetence of former counsel must be sufficiently specific and clearly supported by the evidence.Note 45

8.4.1.3. Reasonable opportunity for counsel to respond

Where a person intends to make an allegation of counsel incompetence, they must notify counsel and provide counsel with a reasonable opportunity to respond to the allegation.Note 46 The specific procedural steps to be followed are set out in section 2.3.1 of the IRB’s Practice Notice - Allegations Against Former Counsel,Note 47 issued in 2018.

8.4.1.4. Immigration and Refugee Board's Practice Notice on Allegations Against Former Counsel

The IRB’s Practice Notice - Allegations Against Former Counsel outlines the steps to be followed where a person who is the subject of an IRB proceeding alleges that their former counsel (i.e. a lawyer, immigration consultant, or other person who is entitled to represent a person for a fee or other consideration at an IRB proceeding) did not provide adequate representation in that proceeding or in any previous proceeding.

Regarding proceedings before the RAD, this Practice Notice specifically provides as follows:

  • When a proceeding is ongoing, the person must provide former counsel with a copy of the appellant’s memorandum or other document in the RAD proceedings that contain the allegations.Note 48
  • When the proceeding is completed and the person is filing an application to reopen the appeal, the person must provide former counsel with a copy of the application to reopen.Note 49
  • In both circumstances, the person must provide former counsel with (i) written notice allowing ten days to respond, (ii) a release of any privilege, including solicitor-client privilege, to the extent necessary to allow former counsel to respond to the allegations; and (iii) a copy of the Practice Notice. The RAD must be provided with copies of these documents, as well as proof that they have been provided to the former counsel.Note 50
  • Former counsel who intends to respond to the allegations must provide a written response to the person, to the RAD, and to the Minister (if the Minister is a party to the RAD proceedings), no later than ten days after receiving the allegations.Note 51
  • The person may provide a written reply to the RAD, accompanied by proof that this reply was first provided to former counsel and to the Minister (if the Minister is a party to the proceeding). The written reply must be provided no later than five days after the person received former counsel’s response.Note 52

This Practice Notice does not require the person to make a complaint about their former counsel to a provincial law society or other body that is responsible for governing or investigating counsel conduct. However, if the person has made such a complaint, the person must provide the RAD with a copy of the complaint made. The person must also immediately inform the RAD of the status of that complaint, continue to notify the RAD of any changes to the status of the complaint, and provide the RAD with a copy of any decision regarding the complaint.Note 53

Failure to comply with this Practice Notice is not enough, on its own, to dismiss the allegation against former counsel.Note 54 Compliance with this Practice Notice, including the notice requirement, is but one factor the RAD may consider in deciding whether allegations against former counsel are credible. If there is evidence of counsel incompetence on the record, the RAD should assess this evidence in deciding whether allegations against former counsel are well-founded.Note 55 Results of disciplinary proceedings by the regulatory body are not binding on the court or on the IRB.Note 56

8.4.2. Type of allegations of counsel incompetence

The Federal Court found that allegations of counsel incompetence were well-founded in the following situations:

  • giving advice that resulted in omissions or errors or other problems in the Basis of Claim Form (BoC);Note 57
  • failing to assist the person in filling out their personal information form or BoC;Note 58
  • failing to prepare the claimant in advance of the hearing;Note 59
  • failing to provide critical evidence and misleading the tribunal about the absence of this critical evidence;Note 60
  • the cumulative impact of conduct, including: letting a translator, who acted like a lawyer, fill out the BOCs; counsel failing to provide the person with the extent of a Minister's intervention and the extent of similarities between their narrative and that of other claimants; and counsel advising the person not to file an amended BOC;Note 61
  • improper translation or failing to translate documents in a timely manner;Note 62
  • failing to make submissions (especially with respect to determinative issues), advocate or question the claimant;Note 63
  • the cumulative impact of filing deficient written submissions and evidence on an application to re-open, along with prior conduct dating back to the commencement of counsel's retainer such as failing to file a request for an extension of time to file the BOC in a timely manner;Note 64
  • representing the person, despite being unauthorized and unfamiliar with the case;Note 65
  • arguing with member/exhibiting unprofessional behaviour; orNote 66
  • failing to show up for the hearing (and failing to communicate with Board);Note 67

The Federal Court found that allegations of counsel incompetence were not well founded in the following situations:

  • failing to provide any evidence to support allegations of counsel incompetence.Note 68
  • failing to call a witness.Note 69
  • providing arguments that are unable to persuade the RAD of an error.Note 70
  • retaining counsel despite being aware of their incompetence and because key negative credibility findings were unrelated to counsel conduct.Note 71
  • the alleged incompetence did not explain the majority of deficiencies noted by the RPD.Note 72
  • failing to apply to the RPD to designate claimants as vulnerable persons and to place video evidence before the RPD.Note 73
  • failing to review identity documents before they are submitted.Note 74
  • clearly indicating that counsel was not representing the person and that the person required new counsel.Note 75
  • failing to file a complaint against former counsel and failing to provide counsel with an opportunity to respond.Note 76
  • counsel’s alleged omission, relating to the absence of documentary evidence regarding proof of employment, had no impact on the outcome of the claim, which was based on the finding of a viable IFA.Note 77

In some cases, the remedy for counsel incompetence is a new hearing before the RPD.Note 78 In other cases, it may be possible for the RAD to confirm or substitute the RPD decision despite counsel’s incompetence.Note 79

8.4.3. Remedying counsel incompetence before the Refugee Appeal Division

The failure to observe a principle of natural justice is a basis upon which the RAD may grant an application to re-open an appeal or to reinstate a withdrawn appeal.Note 80 Sometimes allegations against former counsel are made within the context of both types of applications and RAD members are called upon to decide whether counsel acted with incompetence.Note 81

8.5. Adequacy of interpretation

Sometimes the quality of the interpretation offered at the RPD hearing is challenged before the RAD and the Federal Court. This section discusses what criteria must be met for the interpretation to be considered adequate and the circumstances under which adequate interpretation has been waived by the person.

8.5.1. Test for adequate interpretation

Section 14 of the Charter does not require “perfection” of interpretation; the key is whether there was linguistic understanding.Note 82 Put another way, the question is whether the individual was allowed to tell their story and to be understood.Note 83

Per the Supreme Court’s decision in Tran, adequate interpretation must be continuous, precise, competent, impartial, and contemporaneous.Note 84

Interpretation is continuous where there are no breaks or interruptions in interpretation.Note 85 Interpretation must be precise: it must be as close as can be, word-for-word and idea-for-idea.Note 86 In order to be impartial, the interpretation should be objective and unbiased. Regarding competency, the Supreme Court indicated in Tran that “interpretation must be of a high enough quality to ensure that justice is done and seen to be done.” At a minimum, interpreters must be sworn by taking the interpreter’s oath before beginning to interpret the proceeding.Note 87 Finally, regarding the need for contemporaneous translation, the Supreme Court held in Tran that consecutive interpretation is the better practice as compared to simultaneous translation.

The person is responsible for explaining how the translation was inadequate with specific examples from the hearing. The RAD is not obliged to reach out to the person to request specific examples if none are provided in the appeal record.Note 88

Once a person establishes that there was a real and significant translation error, he or she is not required to also demonstrate that the error underpinned a key finding before the RPD decision can be set aside.Note 89 However, the errors must be serious and non-trivial, and they must have affected the individual’s ability to present their allegations and answer questions.Note 90

In some cases, the remedy for inadequate interpretation is a new hearing.Note 91

In other cases, where the translation errors are minor, it may be possible for the RAD to confirm or substitute the RPD decision despite the interpretation issues. For example, in Gebremedhin, the Federal Court concluded that the RAD’s own assessment with the correct translations was reasonable.Note 92

For more information, please see Chapter 7 on Remedies.

8.5.2. Waiver

For questions related to the quality of the interpretation, parties must raise the quality of interpretation at the first possible moment unless there are exceptional circumstances for not doing so.Note 93 It will be a question of fact in each case whether it is reasonable to expect a complaint to be made.Note 94

For example, in Aldarwish, the Federal Court found that the RAD reasonably concluded that the person and her counsel failed to raise questions about the quality of interpretation at the earliest reasonable opportunity.Note 95 In this case, the RPD hearing occurred over 2 sittings. At the beginning of the first hearing, the RPD member put the person on notice to raise concerns about interpretation if she had any difficulty communicating with the interpreter. At the beginning of the second sitting, the person testified, “I expect I am anticipating that I am more lucky this time with the interpreter.” In its waiver analysis, the RAD noted that the person’s counsel would have heard the foregoing statement that she was “unlucky”. This would have provided a basis during the second hearing to raise the issue to investigate the quality of the translation during the first hearing. Additionally, if the client had such concerns that are intended to have been described being unlucky with the first interpreter, she should have addressed them with her counsel who thereafter could have raised them with the RPD member.

8.6. Recordings and transcripts

In conducting its independent review of the RPD record, the RAD reviews the audio recording of the hearing before the RPD and/or reads the transcript of the RPD.Note 96 Sometimes administrative errors occur and recordings and/or transcripts of the RPD hearing are not available. This section sets out the applicable legal test to apply where RAD members are required to assess whether they are able to properly dispose of the appeal without the missing recording and/or transcript.

Where a CD recording of the RPD hearing is available and has been provided to the person in advance of their appeal, the Court has held that the RAD is not under an obligation to provide the person with transcripts of the hearing. The Court also held that the RAD did not breach procedural fairness by relying on transcripts the person did not have.Note 97

8.6.1. Test to determine whether the absence of a recording or transcript amounts to a breach of procedural fairness

A missing recording or transcript does not automatically lead to a breach of procedural fairness.Note 98 In Canadian Union of Public Employees, Local 301, the Supreme Court of Canada set out the test for determining when a missing transcript results in a breach of procedural fairness: Does the record allow the decision maker to properly dispose of the application for appeal or review? If so, the absence of a transcript will not violate the rules of natural justice.Note 99 On the other hand, if the court cannot dispose of an application before it because of the absence of a transcript, this will violate the rules of natural justice.Note 100

While the RAD’s role on appeal differs from that of the Federal Court on judicial review, this legal test applies to the RAD in determining whether there is a breach of procedural fairness as a result of missing recordings or transcripts of the RPD hearing.Note 101

In determining whether the absence of a recording or transcript amounts to a breach of procedural unfairness, the case law establishes a number of factors that may be considered, such as:

  • the grounds for review advanced;
  • the importance of the impugned findings to the refugee claim;
  • the basis upon which the RPD arrived at its findings;
  • the subject matter of the transcript gaps, and the significance of the transcript gaps to the impugned findings;
  • other means the tribunal used to fill the gaps; and,
  • other means available to the Court to determine what went on at the hearing.Note 102

The Federal Court has held that an adequate review may be possible without a recording in the following circumstances:

  • where the alleged errors relate to a legal error in the member’s analysis or where the errors do not relate to the oral testimony,Note 103
  • where the RPD caught the recording malfunction during the course of the hearing and sought to supplement the record,Note 104 (unless the RPD's supplementation of the record is inaccurate and/or incomplete),Note 105
  • where the person failed to identify the substance of the information missing from the recordings nor explain why the missing information was critical to addressing an issue central to their claim and to the Court's ability to properly review the RAD's decision,Note 106
  • where the only portion of the missing recording relate to counsel’s closing arguments before the RPD,Note 107 or
  • where the decision being reviewed turns on the decision-maker’s assessment of a more “objective” factor.Note 108

The Federal Court has held that an adequate review may not be possible without a recording or transcript in the following circumstances:

  • where the alleged errors relate to credibility assessment,Note 109
  • where the alleged errors relate to interpretation errors,Note 110
  • where the alleged errors relate to the misapprehension of testimony,Note 111 or
  • where the conduct of the member is at issue.Note 112

Where the RAD finds that a missing recording or transcript breaches procedural fairness, the remedy may be to order a new hearing at the RPD.Note 113 In other cases, it may be possible for the RAD to confirm or substitute the RPD decision on different grounds despite the missing recording or transcript.Note 114

For more information, please see Chapter 7 on Remedies.

8.7. Disclosure by the Refugee Appeal Division and opportunity to provide submissions

In assessing appeals, the RAD must sometimes disclose information such as new national documentation packages, extrinsic evidence or new issues to the parties and allow parties to provide submissions. The following section addresses relevant jurisprudence, policies, and rules that the RAD must follow in deciding whether new information or issues must be disclosed.

8.7.1. Disclosing the National Documentation Package (NDP)

The RAD has a duty to consider information contained in the NDP.Note 115 The RAD must consider the most recent NDP in its assessment of forward-looking risk.Note 116

In certain circumstances, procedural fairness will require the RAD to notify the parties of its intention to rely upon a new version of the relevant NDP.

In Zhang,Note 117 the RAD had relied upon a version of the NDP for China that arose after the persons perfected their appeal. The pertinent section had been updated to include information that was not in the version of the NDP that was available to the persons at the time their appeal was perfected. The Federal Court found that the RAD breached procedural fairness by failing to give the persons an opportunity to respond to the new information. In her reasons, Justice Kane wrote:

… [Where] recent information arises after [a person] has perfected their appeal and made their submissions and that information is different and shows a change in the general country conditions, the RAD has a duty to advise the [person] that it is relying on that information.Note 118

On the facts of the case, Justice Kane considered whether the more recent information was sufficiently different, novel and significant compared to the information available to the persons at the time they perfected their appeal so as to require the RAD to give the persons an opportunity to respond to it.Note 119 She concluded that the more recent information appeared to minimize the risk the persons would face in China, depending upon the application of certain factors, and therefore the persons should have been given an opportunity to respond to it.Note 120

In Lin,Note 121 the RAD had notified the person that it would be “making reference” to particular sections of a version of the relevant NDP that post-dated the perfection of the appeal and invited him to make submissions. The person then requested that the RAD specify how it intended to rely upon those sections, however the RAD issued its decision without responding to that request. The Federal Court held that, contrary to the person’s position, the RAD did not breach its duty of fairness and its notice to the person was sufficient. In his reasons, Justice Ahmed wrote:

None of the authorities cited in Zhang stand for the principle that the duty of fairness requires a decision-maker to disclose how or what segments of the NDP it intends to rely upon […]. Rather, a decision-maker breaches its duty of fairness by relying on documentary evidence that a claimant is not aware of, nor deemed to be aware of […]. [References omitted.]Note 122

Similarly in Sarmiento Florez,Note 123 the Federal Court found that the persons were provided with an opportunity to present their case, fully and fairly, when the RAD gave the persons written notice that it was going to use an updated version of the NDP and invited the persons to provide written submissions about a specific document in the NDP. While the persons argued that the purpose of the notice was not clear and the RAD should have been more specific in order to be fair, the Federal Court found that counsel for the persons did in fact provide submissions and did not complain at the time that the notice or its purpose were unclear.Note 124

8.7.2. Disclosing additional documentary evidence

The RAD sometimes relies on extrinsic evidence in disposing appeals. Extrinsic evidence is evidence that the parties are not aware of because it comes from an outside source.Note 125 In other words, it is evidence that does not form part of the RPD record,Note 126 the appellant’s recordNote 127 or the Minister’s evidence.Note 128 For example, the RAD may wish to rely on newspaper articles, social media posts, websites, maps, or other documents that are not on the record but that are relevant to the appeal.

Generally, the additional documentary evidence relied on by the RAD must be disclosed to the parties and the parties must be provided with an opportunity to respond.Note 129 In addition, the RAD must gather and disclose information for its proceedings in conformity with the Chairperson’s Instructions for Gathering and Disclosing Information for Refugee Appeal Division Proceedings.Note 130

However, in Olanrewaju, the Court held that the RAD's google search of an address was not extrinsic evidence and did not require disclosure. In doing so, the Court relied upon a line of authority in the context of the judicial review of decisions of an immigration officer that publicly available information is not extrinsic evidence so long as it is not novel or significant.Note 131

8.7.3. Judicial notice and specialized knowledge

The RAD may also take judicial notice of any facts that may be judicially noticed and of any other generally recognized facts and any information or opinion that is within its specialized knowledge.Note 132

Judicial notice requires disclosure to the parties and the parties must be provided with an opportunity to respond.Note 133

Before using any information or opinion that is within its specialized knowledge, the RAD must notify the parties and give them an opportunity to make written representations on the reliability and use of the information or opinion and provide written evidence in support of their representations.Note 134

For more information, please refer to Chapter 10 of the reference paper on Weighing Evidence (Chapter 10: Judicial notice and specialized knowledge).

8.7.4. Disclosing new issues at the Refugee Appeal Division

New issues are legally and factually distinct from the grounds of appeal raised by the parties.Note 135 In other words, a new issue is a question that would take the person or the Minister by surprise.

Where a new issue is raised by the RAD for the first time on appeal, notice must be provided to the person and the Minister, even if the Minister is not a party to the proceedings.Note 136 In response to the notice, the person, or the Minister, if the Minister decides to intervene, may provide submissions and new evidence.

For a detailed examination of the case law on new issues, please refer to Chapter 3: New Issues.

8.8. Bias

IRB members are expected to render decisions in an independent and impartial manner. RAD members may be called upon to decide whether an RPD member has exhibited bias or an appearance of bias. The impartiality of RAD members may also be subject to challenge. This section discusses situations in which the Federal Court has considered bias allegations against RPD or RAD members.

8.8.1. Test to determine a reasonable apprehension of bias

The presence of bias or a reasonable apprehension of bias can, if established, constitute a breach procedural fairness by depriving a party of a fair hearing.Note 137

The applicable test to determine the existence of a reasonable apprehension of bias has been articulated by the Supreme Court of Canada as follows:

… the apprehension of bias must be a reasonable one, held by reasonable and right minded persons, applying themselves to the question and obtaining thereon the required information… [The] test is “what would an informed person, viewing the matter realistically and practically – and having thought the matter through – conclude. Would he think that it is more likely than not that [the decision-maker], whether consciously or unconsciously, would not decide fairly.”Note 138

The burden is on the party alleging bias to establish a reasonable apprehension of bias on a balance of probabilities.Note 139

While the assessment requires a real likelihood or probability of bias, it is unnecessary to demonstrate that a decision-maker is actually biased. An appearance of bias is sufficient.Note 140

The threshold to establish a reasonable apprehension of bias is a high oneNote 141 and the grounds for apprehension of bias must be substantial.Note 142 Decision-makers are presumed to be impartial.Note 143 Allegations of bias must be supported by material evidence and cannot rest on mere suspicion, insinuations or impressions of a party or their counsel.Note 144

As with procedural fairness issues generally, parties seeking to raise bias allegations are expected to do so at the earliest available opportunity.Note 145

8.8.2. The Refugee Appeal Division's review of allegations of bias at the Refugee Protection Division

The RAD can hear and decide arguments as to whether RPD conduct establishes bias or a reasonable apprehension of bias and, thus, a breach of procedural fairness.

Where the RAD finds that the conduct or decision of the RPD member raises a reasonable apprehension of bias, the RAD will determine whether it is possible to cure the RPD’s error and make a determination on the refugee claim.Note 146

The Federal Court has found that in considering allegations of bias involving the conduct or questioning of RPD members during a hearing, the RAD should review not only the written transcript of the hearing but also the audio recording.Note 147

Where the RAD is unable to cure the RPD’s error, and where the statutory criteria are met for redetermination, a finding of bias on the part of the RPD member may result in the matter being remitted to the RPD for reconsideration.Note 148

Both RAD and RPD members can hear applications made on the grounds of a reasonable apprehension of bias involving themselves as decision-makers, including applications for decision-makers to recuse themselves.Note 149 The RAD can review the RPD member’s decisions on recusal applications. Members may also recuse themselves voluntarily if, for instance, they perceive that they would be incapable of considering a claim impartially.

8.8.3. Allegations of member bias

8.8.3.1. Member questioning

Rigorous, “extensive and energetic” questioning will not, on its own, give rise to a reasonable apprehension of bias,Note 150 nor will questioning that indicates that the member had formed merely a tentative view of the evidence.Note 151 Members must sometimes ask difficult questions that may be upsetting for the person. This, in of itself, is not sufficient to support an allegation of a reasonable apprehension of bias.Note 152

The mere existence of a tense atmosphere in the hearing room, expressions of momentary impatience, an ill-chosen or insensitive question, or the use of harsh or sarcastic language will, on their own, generally be insufficient to establish a reasonable apprehension of bias.Note 153 The panel’s questioning may demonstrate a lack of professionalism without necessarily giving rise to a reasonable apprehension of bias.Note 154

Disparaging and sarcastic commentary could demonstrate a loss of objectivity, which undermines both the appearance and the fact of impartiality resulting in a breach of procedural fairness.Note 155

The Federal Court has also found that while the tribunal is entitled to “cross-examine” a claimant, this cannot be confused with harassing the claimant.Note 156

8.8.3.2. Member research

Paragraph 170(a) of IRPA provides that the RPD can “inquire into any matter it considers relevant to establishing whether a claim is well-founded.” The Federal Court of Appeal has acknowledged the Board’s inquisitorial role and its central role in fact-finding.Note 157 As such, a member may introduce documents to the claimant during the hearing without giving rise to a reasonable apprehension of bias.Note 158

However, a member’s initiation of a “quest for evidence” may expose them to a charge of bias, where the member’s motive in examining the claimant on that evidence cannot be said to be to clarify or reconcile inconsistent testimony.Note 159

8.8.3.3. Member demeanor

Board members are required to demonstrate patience, respect and restraint.Note 160 While, as stated above, sarcastic and harsh language will typically not be sufficient, on its own, to demonstrate that a member lacks impartiality, the adjudicative process requires an appropriate tone and demeanor in order to ensure that the member is not seen to have reached a decision prematurely.Note 161 Fairness does not permit intrusive, insensitive, intimidating, harassing, unwarranted, or highly irrelevant interventions by the member which are capable of giving the impression that the member was biased.Note 162

The Court has also recognized the contribution of counsel to the hearing room environment. The Court declined to find a reasonable apprehension of bias where, although the RPD member presented herself as upset and possibly angry during the hearing, almost all of the impugned verbal exchanges were between the member and the person’s counsel. The Court noted that counsel was asked on at least two occasions to be more respectful and must bear some responsibility for the difficulties that arose during the hearing.Note 163

8.8.3.4. Use of "Boilerplate" in reasons

The Federal Court has found that the RPD’s use of “boilerplate” passages is acceptable when they address the actual claim being considered, and the previous analysis fits the situation of the claimant before it.Note 164

However, where reasons are taken virtually word for word from an earlier decision, this may give rise to a finding that the decision maker acted without due care and attention to the record, especially where accompanied by errors in referring to the parties.Note 165

8.8.3.5. Oral reasons

Procedural fairness does not always require a lengthy period of deliberation on the part of the decision-maker before the delivery of oral reasons for decision. A short deliberation period does not necessarily mean that the decision-maker formed an opinion before considering the parties’ evidence and arguments,Note 166 particularly where the RPD accounted for the person’s testimony and arguments in its reasons.Note 167

However, the Federal Court has cautioned that a decision-maker’s haste may indicate that the person did not receive a fair hearing. For instance, the Federal Court found a breach of procedural fairness where the RPD read extensive reasons for decision immediately at the conclusion of the hearing.Note 168

In another case, the person alleged a reasonable apprehension of bias because the member had referred, during the hearing, to a document that had the format and look of a decision.Note 169 The Federal Court concluded that the fact that, prior to the hearing, the member had prepared a [translation] “draft decision” that looked like a decision would give rise to serious concerns about the member’s impartiality in the mind of a reasonable, right minded observer who was well informed on the subject. The Federal Court was of the opinion that, although it was certain that the member had acted in good faith, appearances were against him, and this fundamental flaw was sufficient in itself to set aside the decision.

8.8.3.6. Interactions between member and counsel

Discord between members and counsel is not necessarily indicative of an appearance of bias.Note 170

8.8.3.7. Improper comments

The following are examples in which the Federal Court has found that comments of the RPD member have been so improper as to give rise to a reasonable apprehension of bias:

  • The member pointed out that the claimant, who alleged that she had been raped, was not emotional when testifying about the incident, and also expressed surprise that the claimant could have forgotten to mention in her narrative that she had been raped twice. The Court found that the member’s comments were not only sexist but displayed an unacceptable lack of sensitivity and compassion.Note 171
  • The member commented that the claimant, who alleged persecution in Russia on the basis of her Jewish identity, did not have a Jewish appearance.Note 172
  • The member commented on the claimant’s “effeminacy” or lack thereof, when assessing his claim, which he had advanced on the basis of his sexual orientation.Note 173
  • The member commented that children sexually abused by male relatives do not suffer abuse or persecution if they are homosexual.Note 174

However, instances of levity that reflected the personal style of the member, and when examined in their context, did not give rise to an apprehension of bias and did not prevent or discourage the claimants from placing their accounts before the Board.Note 175

8.8.3.8. Member's previous decisions in a related matter

Members develop expertise with respect to certain countries and certain issues and are bound to come across cases with similar fact patterns, and similar legal issues. Even if a decision-maker had erred in law in another case, there would still be no basis for asserting bias.

The Federal Court has declined to find an appearance of bias where the member had been part of a panel that rejected the refugee claim of the person’s friend, with whom the person had travelled to Canada.Note 176

The Federal Court has also found that the fact that the member who heard the person’s claim for refugee protection was the same person who had earlier heard and rendered a negative decision in connection with his spouse’s claim for refugee protection did not in itself create a reasonable apprehension of bias.Note 177

Nor will the fact that the member was the same individual who had previously declared the person’s original claim abandoned, in and of itself, give rise to a reasonable apprehension of bias requiring the member to recuse himself.Note 178

The Federal Court has also found no reasonable apprehension of bias where the member ordered a de novo hearing because of interpretation issues but remained the decision-maker in the claim.Note 179 The Federal Court found that there was no reasonable apprehension of bias when the member refused to recuse himself from presiding at the de novo hearing. It was open to the member to choose what questions to put to the person at the de novo hearing and the fact that the member did not ask the person the same questions again did not raise a reasonable apprehension of bias.

8.8.3.9. Member's personal background

The Courts have also considered bias arguments on the basis of the decision-maker’s personal background, history or characteristics.

The alleged religious beliefs of a decision maker are irrelevant to the issue of bias.Note 180 Moreover, the mere fact that a decision-maker is originally from the same country as the refugee status claimant will not suffice to justify a reasonable fear of bias.Note 181

However, in a subsequent case involving the same member, the Court considered evidence that the member had, in the past, expressed the view that Ghana did not produce legitimate refugees.Note 182 The Court found that an informed person would think it more likely than not that the member might decide the issue unfairly.

8.8.3.10. Member's acceptance rates

The Federal Court has repeatedly found that statistics about acceptance rates of members do not, in and of themselves, give rise to a reasonable apprehension of bias.Note 183

Where the person cannot point to any instance of bias or prejudice on the part of the member, the numbers alone as presented by the person are insufficient. Mere suspicion based on “rates” has not been found to meet the applicable standard of the well-informed individual considering the matter in depth in a realistic and practical way.Note 184

The Federal Court has also declined to find an appearance of bias where the person argued that the member’s acceptance rates for claimants of the person’s country of origin was below the national acceptance rate for claimants from that country, without any analysis of the figures they submitted to support their allegation of bias.Note 185

8.8.3.11. Prejudicial information seen by member

The Federal Court has also found that the member’s viewing of evidence for the purpose of assessing its admissibility does not, in and of itself, establish an appearance of bias and does not necessarily mean that the panel was relying on that evidence.Note 186

In addition, it is not a violation of the principles of natural justice for the member to read a previous adverse decision before hearing a refugee claim de novo, where there is no evidence that the second panel prejudged the case or failed to recognize that this was a de novo hearing.Note 187

8.8.3.12. Notice to the Minister

The Federal Court has declined to find an appearance of bias where the RPD member informed the Minister of the possible application of Article 1E of the Refugee Convention owing to the person’s Ethiopian permanent residence and to inform the Minister that the authenticity of the documents that the person presented was in question. Although the person argued that the RPD’s letter to the Minister showed a reasonable apprehension of bias and that the language showed that a decision on exclusion had already been made, the Federal Court held that the RPD acted in accordance with the Rules in informing the Minister both times, and that there was nothing in its conduct to support the allegation that it had already made a determination that Article 1E applied.Note 188

8.8.3.13. Chairperson's Guidelines and Jurisprudential Guides

The Chairperson’s Guidelines and Jurisprudential Guides are policy instruments identified by the Board that are applied in decision-making.

Chairperson’s Guidelines are issued under the authority given in paragraph 159(1)(h) of the IRPA, and provide guiding principles for adjudicating and managing cases. IRB members are expected to follow the Chairperson’s Guidelines unless compelling or exceptional reasons exist to depart from them.Note 189

Jurisprudential Guides are IRB decisions that are well-written, detailed and contain persuasive reasoning. They are aimed at facilitating fair decision-making consistent with the Board’s statutory obligation to deal with all proceedings before it as informally and quickly as the circumstances and the considerations of fairness and natural justice permit. The Policy on the Use of Chairperson’s Guidelines and Jurisprudential Guides indicates that neither the Guidelines nor the Jurisprudential Guides are binding and members remain free to reach their own conclusions based on the facts of the case.

The Federal Court of Appeal recently confirmed that the Chairperson has the authority under paragraph 159(1)(h) of the IRPA to issue jurisprudential guides that include factual determinations.Note 190 The Court of Appeal also confirmed that none of the Jurisprudential Guides before it improperly encroached upon members’ adjudicative independence, and found no reasonable apprehension of bias related to the use of the Jurisprudential Guides, for three principal reasons:

  1. the Court distinguished factual determinations which solely required consideration of evidence specific to the circumstances of the parties from the special nature of the factual determinations at issue in the JGs, which involved the review of country condition evidence;
  2. the public nature of the JGs is to be contrasted with the behind-closed-doors consultations between members; and
  3. IRB members remain free to decide cases based on their own assessment of the facts and of the evidence before them.Note 191

The member should have stood back and dispassionately considered the effect this dispute with counsel was having on the hearing. An informed and reasonable observer would conclude that it was more likely than not that the claim would not be decided fairly in the circumstances. In Saint-Eustache v. Canada (Minister of Citizenship and Immigration), 2012 FC 511 (CanLII), the Court found a reasonable apprehension of bias where, without explanation, the RPD member refused counsel’s recusal request that was made on the basis of a history of animosity between the member and counsel, criticized counsel for disrupting the conduct of the hearing, and invited her to withdraw if she did not want the hearing to proceed. The Court noted that the member placed the person and his counsel in an unacceptable position. He was required to explain the reasoning behind his refusal of the recusal request by counsel instead of attacking her, and his conduct created an untenable environment for the person, who was forced to choose between maintaining his counsel and running the risk of offending the member.
