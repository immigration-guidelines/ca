Responses to Information Requests

Page Content

​​​​​​​Responses to Information Requests (RIRs) are research reports on country conditions. They are requested by IRB decision-makers.

The database contains a seven-year archive of English and French RIRs. Earlier RIRs may be found on the European Country of Origin Information Network website.

RIR​s published by the IRB on its website may have attachments that are inaccessible due to technical constraints and may include translations of documents originally written in languages other than English or French. To obtain a copy of such attachments and/or translated version of the RIR attachments, please email us.​

Disclaimer

Responses to Information Requests (RIRs) cite publicly accessible information available at the time of publication and within time constraints. A list of references and additional sources consulted are included in each RIR. Sources cited are considered the most current information available as of the date of the RIR.            

RIRs are not, and do not purport to be, conclusive as to the merit of any particular claim for refugee protection. Rather, they are intended to support the refugee determination process. More information on the methodology used by the Research Directorate can be found here.          

The assessment and weight to be given to the information in the RIRs are the responsibility of independent IRB members (decision-makers) after considering the evidence and arguments presented by the parties.           

The information presented in RIRs solely reflects the views and perspectives of the sources cited and does not necessarily reflect the position of the IRB or the Government of Canada.          

28 May 2026

BGD202589.E

Bangladesh: The Bangladesh Awami League (AL), including its activities, structure, leaders, membership, factions, and associated organizations; membership documents; treatment of members and supporters by authorities (2024–May 2026)

Research Directorate, Immigration and Refugee Board of Canada

1. The AL

1.1. Overview

A report by Australia's Department of Foreign Affairs and Trade (DFAT) states that the AL won Bangladesh [national] elections in 2014, 2018, and 2024 (Australia 2025-07-23, para. 2.4). The same source reports that following mass anti-government protests in 2024, Sheikh Hasina, the leader of the AL, fled in August 2024, and the party was banned in May 2025 (Australia 2025-07-23, para. 2.5, 2.7). A fact-finding report by the UN Office of the High Commissioner for Human Rights (OHCHR) on the protests of July and August 2024, which resulted in an estimated 1,400 deaths, states that

there are reasonable grounds to believe that the former Government and its security and intelligence apparatus, together with violent elements associated with the Awami League, systematically engaged in serious human rights violations, including hundreds of extrajudicial killings, other use of force violations involving serious injuries to thousands of protesters, extensive arbitrary arrest and detention, and torture and other forms of ill-treatment. (UN 2025-02-12, i, iii)

The website of the Bangladesh Election Commission indicates that the AL is "suspended" (Bangladesh n.d.). Sources report that the AL was banned from participating in Bangladesh's February 2026 election (BBC 2026-02-13; Al Jazeera 2026-02-15; The Guardian 2026-02-13). According to the same sources, the Bangladesh Nationalist Party (BNP) won the 2026 election, taking 212 seats (BBC 2026-02-13; Al Jazeera 2026-02-15; The Guardian 2026-02-13). For information on the BNP, see Response to Information Request BGD202582 of May 2026.

1.2. Leadership and Structure

According to sources, Sheikh Hasina became leader of the AL in 1981 (BBC 2025-11-17; AL 2018-09-27; Political Handbook of the World 2025, 21). Hasina was prime minister from 1996 to 2001, and from 2009 to 2024 (Political Handbook of the World 2025, 4, 10; UK 2025-01-23, 4, 9). Sources report that in August 2024, following anti-government protests, Hasina fled the country (ABC News 2024-08-05; BBC 2025-11-17; Reuters 2025-11-17) to India (BBC 2025-11-17; Reuters 2025-11-17). The Guardian reports that Hasina is staying in a "secret hideout" in Delhi (2026-02-03). Freedom House states that many senior AL officials went into hiding following Hasina's resignation (2025-02-26, Sec. A1). The Guardian reports that following Hasina's departure "[t]housands" of AL party members fled, including "[m]ore than" 600 who escaped to Kolkata and have been hiding out there since (2026-02-03).

Information on the structure of the AL could not be found among the sources consulted by the Research Directorate within the time constraints of this response.

1.3. Membership

Information on AL membership was scarce among the sources consulted by the Research Directorate within the time constraints of this Response. On the AL website, there is an online form that can be completed to join the party (AL n.d.). The membership form on the AL website requires submitting the following information:

  • Full name
  • Gender
  • Date of birth
  • Mobile phone number
  • Email address
  • Division
  • District
  • Constituency (AL n.d.).

1.4. Associated Organizations

1.4.1. Factions

Sources report that the AL's student wing, the Bangladesh Chhatra League (BCL), was banned in 2024 following Hasina's resignation (Freedom House 2025-02-26, Sec. D3; Al Jazeera 2024-10-27). Sources report that the BCL was declared a "'terrorist'" organization by Bangladesh's interim government [1] (Al Jazeera 2024-10-27; The Guardian 2026-02-03) and that its president, Saddam Hussain, "faces multiple charges of sedition and crimes against humanity" (The Guardian 2026-02-03). Al Jazeera reports that members of the BCL are facing "eviction, retribution and even imprisonment for their role in trying to suppress the popular revolt against Hasina and for the rights violations they allegedly committed while she was in power" (2024-10-27). The same source reports, citing the Dhaka Metropolitan Police (DMP), that in the 5 days after the BCL ban came into effect, "[m]ore than 100 student activists" were arrested countrywide, with "at least" 10 of those arrests being individuals from Dhaka (Al Jazeera 2024-10-27). A senior official from the DMP told Al Jazeera that these arrests are based on "'no specific charges'" and that the individuals are "'under suspicion … largely because of their affiliation with'" the BCL; "'[a]lmost all'" arrests are related to the July 2024 anti-government protests (Al Jazeera 2024-10-27). The same Al Jazeera article reports that 4 BCL leaders from Rajshahi University and Dhaka University were arrested in October 2024 while trying to write exams (2024-10-27). Al Jazeera also reports that in September 2024, a BCL leader was killed by a mob in Rajshahi and a BCL "activist" from Jahangirnagar University was beaten to death (2024-10-27).

Al Jazeera indicates that the Bangladesh Krishak League is a pro-AL organization and that Samiul Bashir is its assistant secretary (2025-01-27). Further information on the Bangladesh Krishak League could not be found among the sources consulted by the Research Directorate within the time constraints of this Response.

2. Membership Documents

Information on AL membership documents could not be found among the sources consulted by the Research Directorate within the time constraints of this Response.

3. Treatment of Members and Supporters by Authorities

According to sources, in November 2025, the Bangladesh International Crimes Tribunal convicted former prime minister Sheikh Hasina and former home minister Asaduzzaman Khan Kamal, in absentia, of crimes against humanity and sentenced them to death (Al Jazeera 2025-11-17; Amnesty International 2025-04-28, 95; Human Rights Watch 2025-11-17). The same sources report that the former police chief pled guilty and was sentenced to 5 years in prison (Al Jazeera 2025-11-17; Amnesty International 2025-04-28, 95; Human Rights Watch 2025-11-17).

According to Freedom House, following Hasina's resignation, AL members and leaders "faced significant violence and legal harassment" and leaders "were arrested for crimes based on little evidence" (2025-02-26, Sec. B1, B3). The same source notes that the interim government opened corruption cases targeting former AL government officials (Freedom House 2025-02-26, Sec. C2).

The Business Standard, an English-language newspaper in Bangladesh [2] (The Business Standard n.d.), reports that between August 2024 and September 2025, there were 5,079 former AL members of Parliament (MPs), ministers, party members, and affiliate group members arrested based on "allegations including violent clashes and fatalities during the uprising" (2025-09-05). The source further reports that, during that same period of time, 706 cases were filed at police stations in Dhaka and "nearly" 2,000 total cases were "reported[ly]" filed nationwide (The Business Standard 2025-09-05). The same source notes that Sheikh Hasina, "along with almost all former ministers, state ministers, advisers, MPs, and party leaders, have been named as defendants in these cases" (The Business Standard 2025-09-05). Prothom Alo, a daily newspaper in Bangladesh [3], states that from January to October 2025 the DMP arrested "nearly" 3,000 AL and affiliate activists and leaders "for taking part in flash processions" across Dhaka (2025-10-31).

Freedom House reports that under the interim government, "individuals with past or current affiliations with the AL were harassed, expelled from their jobs, and killed" and that, while the interim government denounced these actions, it was "unable to fully control political retribution" (2025-02-26, Sec. D4). Amnesty International indicates that in December 2025, a journalist was detained under the Anti-Terrorism Act "after a complaint was filed against him and four others for allegedly spreading propaganda in favour of the Awami League" (2025-12-17). Freedom House reports that while "some" protests had taken place "without state restriction" under the interim government, "there were instances in which AL gatherings and supporters were attacked and were not protected by the police" (2025-02-26, Sec. E1).

Prothom Alo reports that following the fall of the AL government, "many lower-tier leaders and activists were arrested"; however, "a significant portion" of those arrested were able to obtain bail "within a few months" (2026-02-28). The same source reports that it has been more difficult for AL members who held significant posts or were former representatives (Prothom Alo 2026-02-28).

The Business Standard reports that in the lead-up to the February 2026 elections "at least" 3,000 AL leaders and activists in Shariatpur officially joined the BNP (2026-02-10). Bdnews24.com, an online newspaper based in Bangladesh [4], states that in Aditmari Upazila, Lalmonirhat, "over a hundred" AL leaders have reportedly joined the local BNP (2026-01-25). Sources report that "[o]ver 100" AL leaders and activists (Barta24.com 2025-11-25) or 5 AL leaders and "many" supporters (SATP 2026-01-06) joined the BNP in Faridpur District (Barta24.com 2025-11-25; SATP 2026-01-06).

The Business Standard quotes an anonymous senior AL leader from Naria as stating that "'[m]any leaders here are facing legal cases or are in jail. We joined the BNP to avoid harassment. Once the situation stabilises, we plan to return to Awami League politics'" (2026-02-10). Prothom Alo indicates that BNP, as well as the Jamaat-e-Islami and Jatiya parties, among others, adopted strategies to attract formerly AL voters (2026-01-09). The same source notes that in some regions AL leaders were being brought into parties and in other regions they were offered promises of legal protection (Prothom Alo 2026-01-09). The source also mentions 2 districts in which the BNP-nominated candidates were previously "associated with" the AL; in a third district, the BNP "dr[ew] criticism" for nominating a businessman "aligned" with the AL (Prothom Alo 2026-01-09).

This Response was prepared after researching publicly accessible information currently available to the Research Directorate within time constraints. This Response is not, and does not purport to be, conclusive as to the merit of any particular claim for refugee protection.
