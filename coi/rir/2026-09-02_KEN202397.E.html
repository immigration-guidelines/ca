​​​​​​​Responses to Information Requests (RIRs) are research reports on country conditions. They are requested by IRB decision-makers.

The database contains a seven-year archive of English and French RIRs. Earlier RIRs may be found on the European Country of Origin Information Network website.

RIR​s published by the IRB on its website may have attachments that are inaccessible due to technical constraints and may include translations of documents originally written in languages other than English or French. To obtain a copy of such attachments and/or translated version of the RIR attachments, please email us.​

Responses to Information Requests (RIRs) cite publicly accessible information available at the time of publication and within time constraints. A list of references and additional sources consulted are included in each RIR. Sources cited are considered the most current information available as of the date of the RIR.            

RIRs are not, and do not purport to be, conclusive as to the merit of any particular claim for refugee protection. Rather, they are intended to support the refugee determination process. More information on the methodology used by the Research Directorate can be found here.          

The assessment and weight to be given to the information in the RIRs are the responsibility of independent IRB members (decision-makers) after considering the evidence and arguments presented by the parties.           

The information presented in RIRs solely reflects the views and perspectives of the sources cited and does not necessarily reflect the position of the IRB or the Government of Canada.          

2 September 2026

KEN202397.E

Kenya: National identity cards, including content, appearance, security features, and samples; challenges to obtaining the card; requirements and procedures to obtain or replace the card from within the country and from abroad; fraudulent identity cards; whether genuine identity cards could be obtained through fraudulent means; cases of state and non-state actors tracking individuals using their national identity cards (2023–January 2026)

Research Directorate, Immigration and Refugee Board of Canada

1. Overview

Sources indicate that Kenyans aged 18 or older are eligible for a national ID card (Law practitioner 2025-09-19; Aratek 2024-11-25; Professor 2025-08-26). The Registration of Persons Act provides the following:

6. Registration

(1) Every person who attains or has attained the age of eighteen years and is unregistered shall be liable to registration under this Act and shall, within ninety days of attaining that age, present himself before a registration officer and register himself by giving to the registration officer the particulars specified in subsection (1) of section 5, and for that purpose shall permit his finger and thumb or toe or palm impressions to be recorded in any manner prescribed.

…

9. Registration officer to issue identity cards

(1) Upon the registration of a person under this Act, the registration officer shall within a period of thirty days from the date of registration issue an identity card in the prescribed form to that person.

… (Kenya 1947)

According to Freedom House, a national ID card is required to vote (2025-02-26, Sec. B4).

2. National ID Cards, Including Content, Appearance, and Security Features

Sources indicate that there are 3 generations of national ID cards in Kenya with the third-generation card, known as the Maisha card, functioning as part of a broader digital identity program (Nasubo 2024-12-05) or "ecosystem" (Amnesty International 2024-09-16, 6). The same sources note that the Maisha card replaces the ["traditional" (Nasubo 2024-12-05)] physical ID (Amnesty International 2024-09-16, 6; Nasubo 2024-12-05). A 2024 report by Amnesty International Kenya explains that the identity ecosystem that is being implemented includes the following:

  • the Maisha Namba, a "Unique Personal Identifier (UPI)" number which is "assigned at birth registration and will be used until death registration";
  • the Maisha card, the third-generation card, which "includes enhanced security features such as the microprocessor electronic chip";
  • the digital ID, a "virtual representation of the physical ID"; and
  • the National Population Master Register, a "single integrated register" consolidating "all existing government databases" (2024-09-16, 6).

In correspondence with the Research Directorate, a Nairobi-based law practitioner, whose practice areas include conveyancing, stated that first-generation cards may still be in circulation but are limited in number and the second-generation cards are held largely by "millennials and older persons" (Law practitioner 2025-09-19). In correspondence with the Research Directorate, a professor at the School of International Development and Global Studies at the University of Ottawa, who has conducted research in Kenya, stated that the second-generation cards remain the "most common" and "some" first-generation cards may still be in circulation (Professor 2025-08-26).

Sources note that due to problems with the roll out of the Maisha card, not all applicants received ID cards (BiometricUpdate.com 2024-02-07; law practitioner 2025-09-19). The Citizen Digital, a Kenyan online news source, cites a government press release as stating that as of 26 August 2024, 476,167 IDs have not yet been collected by their applicants (2024-08-27). Sources indicate that Nairobi, Kiambu, Nakuru, and Kisumi counties had the highest numbers of uncollected IDs (Citizen Digital 2024-08-27; The Kenya Times 2024-08-27).

Drawing on focus group discussions with an undisclosed number of participants from 9 counties across Kenya, the Amnesty International Kenya report notes that an "overwhelming majority" of responses "across all counties" indicated "a significant level of skepticism" toward the new ID system, including around the government's ability to secure personal data, the level of "public participation" behind it, and the "'true' motivations for digital ID as a trend of digital transformation" (2024-09-16, 13, 15). The report also notes a "lack of public awareness" about the ID system, and, relatedly, that some participants reported that they obtained the Maisha card "unintentionally," such as when they were attempting to replace their existing ID (Amnesty International 2024-09-16, 18).

2.1. First-Generation ID Card

The information in the following paragraph was provided in a policy paper on Kenyan digital IDs produced by Amnesty International Kenya and the Centre for Intellectual Property and Information Technology Law (CIPIT) based at Strathmore University in Nairobi:

In 1947, the country's national ID was an identity booklet, also known as a "passbook," and it had the holder's fingerprint but no photo. All men, aged 16 and older, were required by law to be registered. In 1978, after an amendment was made to the Registration of Persons Act, the booklet was replaced with the first-generation paper ID card, which had "subtle security features" as well as the bearer's photo and fingerprint. Additionally, the registration age was raised to 18 years. In 1980, another amendment was made to the Act, allowing women to register for a national ID card (Amnesty International & CIPIT 2024-01-31, 16).

The Professor stated that the first-generation cards are made from laminated green cardstock, containing the bearer's photograph, fingerprint, signature, and a colour image of the Kenyan coat of arms (2025-08-26).

A sample of the first-generation card is available online on the website of Kenya's embassy in Washington, DC (Kenya n.d.a).

2.2. Second-Generation ID Card

Sources indicate that a second-generation ID card came into circulation in 1995 (Amnesty International & CIPIT 2024-01-31, 16; Professor 2025-08-26; Nasubo 2024-12-05). Sources note that it was a laminated card (Amnesty International & CIPIT 2024-01-31, 16; Kemboi 2025-03-12). Amnesty International and CIPIT indicate that it was paper-based and credit-card sized, and featured the bearer's name, sex, date and place of birth, photo, signature, and fingerprint; the date and place of issue; a "sequential 8-digit national ID number;" a 9-digit serial number; and a machine-readable zone on the back, meant for authenticating the card (2024-01-31, 16). Sources indicate that in 2011, the laminated card was upgraded to Teslin [a synthetic medium], but its ["core" (Aratek 2024-11-25; Professor 2025-08-26)] features were maintained (Amnesty International & CIPIT 2024-01-31, 16-17; Aratek 2024-11-25; Professor 2025-08-26).

The information in the following 2 paragraphs was provided in a report on the new identity ecosystem published by Kenya's State Department for Immigration and Citizen Services:

The security features of the second-generation card are "outdated," since the Teslin medium cannot support integrated chips. Additionally, this card does facilitate identification through fingerprints but cannot integrate other biometrics such as facial or iris recognition. Statutory records are maintained by "manual indexing and filing," which is "insecure," "unreliable," and "inefficient."

Maisha cards will replace second-generation ID cards. Individuals can continue to use their second-generation cards; however, one may "wish" to replace it in order to "access the benefits of the 3rd [g]eneration ID" (Kenya [2024], 11−12).

A sample of the second-generation card is available online on the website of Kenya's embassy in Washington, DC (Kenya n.d.a).

2.3. Maisha Card

Sources indicate that the Maisha card and its related ecosystem were launched in November 2023 (The Kenya Times 2024-07-22; Keesing Technologies 2024-02-27; Nasubo 2024-12-05) with the government issuing cards in a pilot program to newborns and first-time applicants at the onset of this launch (Keesing Technologies 2024-02-27).

The Professor stated that the Maisha card is printed on a polycarbonate plastic with an "embedded machine-readable chip" (2025-08-26). In his analysis of the Maisha Namba program published in the Elephant [1], Fred Nasubo, a university lecturer [2], refers to the chip as a "microprocessor electronic chip" (Nasubo 2024-12-05). The Professor stated that the cards feature a variety of inks, both visible and UV-responsive, and that these "printing features" discourage counterfeiting (2025-08-26).

The Professor stated that unlike previous generations of cards, which had no expiry date, the third-generation card expires after 10 years; this "invited criticism" due to "the cost of renewing cards repeatedly over a lifetime, the possibility of state interference in who can renew, and [concerns around] if non-renewed cards would limit voting rights or access to state services" (2025-08-26). Section 2(4) of the Registration of Persons Rules provides the following: "The expiry date in the polycarbonate electronic card relates to the electronic functionality of the card" (Kenya 1949). The Kenya Times, an online news source, cites a statement from the Kenya State Department for Immigration and Citizen Services as indicating that when renewing the card, new biometrics will not be taken, but a new photo will be taken (2024-07-22).

A sample of the third-generation card is available in the First Schedule of the Registration of Persons Rules, which is available online (Kenya 1949).

2.3.1. Digital ID

The State Department for Immigration and Citizen Services notes that the digital ID provides a "virtual representation of your physical ID … to verify and authenticate the identity of individuals or entities in various online transactions and interactions" (Kenya [2024], 14). The Professor indicated that the digital identity program was launched along with the National Population Master Registrar as part of the Maisha Namba system (2025-08-26). The same source added that the digital ID has a QR code that can replace the physical Maisha card when accessing services (Professor 2025-08-26). Sources state that the digital ID can be used for accessing government or private services (Kenya [2024], 17; Professor 2025-08-26), such as banking, health care, and insurance (Professor 2025-08-26). According to the Government of Kenya's website for the digital ID, this ID "typically includes information such as personal attributes, credentials, and authentication methods" (Kenya n.d.b). According to the State Department for Immigration and Citizen Services, linking the digital IDs to a "secure infrastructure," such as the National Public Key Infrastructure [3], "establishes trust in online transactions" (Kenya [2024], 15).

3. Challenges

Drawing on a "limited, exploratory study" which included a literature review, interviews with stakeholders, and interviews and focus groups with 53 Kenyans, a report on Kenya's digital ID by Julie Zollman and 2 co-authors [4] notes that the Maisha Namba follows "multiple failed digital ID attempts that wasted hundreds of millions of dollars in public resources" (Zollman, et al. 2024-08-28b, 2−3, 40). The same source notes that Kenyans "doubt the government's intentions and abilities to execute" the initiative, and "express trepidation" that it will "waste significant resources and achieve very little while passing new costs and risks back down to citizens" (Zollman, et al. 2024-08-28b, iii, 4). A blog post by Leo Kipkogei Kemboi, an economist and programme officer at the Institute of Economic Affairs (IEA Kenya) [5] (IEA Kenya n.d.), states that the Registration of Persons (Amendment) Rules "have addressed privacy concerns by limiting biometric data collection, but serious risks like identity theft and unauthorized surveillance still exist" (Kemboi 2025-03-12).

Kenya's State Department for Immigration and Citizen Services indicates that the Maisha card is being introduced to "improve access to legal identity registration services," as well as to modernize the security features of the national ID card and the data-management processes which support it (Kenya [2024], 11).

Freedom House notes that all voters must have a national ID card, which "hinders historically marginalized groups from obtaining greater access to the political process, particularly the nearly seven million pastoralists from the upper Rift Valley and North Eastern Province" (2025-02-26, Sec. B4).

The information in the following paragraph was provided by the Professor:

In terms of the second-generation card, applications for national ID cards were made in person to the local administration. Due to the distance between local administration centres and rural areas, there is a "higher" proportion of people with IDs in urban areas. Furthermore, due to the historical legal requirement of only men being permitted to apply for national ID cards, it is "slightly more" common for parents not to apply for ID cards for their daughters or for later-born children in large families (2025-08-26).

Bertelsmann Stiftung's Transformation Index (BTI) 2026, which "assesses the transformation toward democracy and a market economy as well as the quality of governance in 137 countries," states that certain groups, such as ethnic Nubians and Somalis, encounter "significant challenges in obtaining identity documents" and "bear additional burdens of proof that can lead to at least temporary denial of complete citizenship rights" (Bertelsmann Stiftung 2026, 2, 7). The Kenya Times notes that residents of Northeastern Kenya have faced "extra" vetting and "ethnic profiling by the State before they acquire birth certificates and national identity cards"; vetting became a requirement in Northern Kenya following a "secessionist conflict in which ethnic Somalis, Muslim Borana" and others "attempted to join Somalia" in the 1960s (2025-03-13).

The Professor reported that there is "no penalty" for not having a national ID card since Kenyans can still access government and private services, such as health coverage, without getting one; however, police will ask for ID cards "in nearly every encounter" and will demand bribes or threaten arrest if one cannot be produced (2025-08-26). The Professor added that this can be "especially problematic for young men and members of ethnic minorities (e.g. Somali or other northern communities like Samburu), who are often 'profiled' by police as politically suspicious" (2025-08-26). Corroborating information could not be found among the sources consulted by the Research Directorate within the time constraints of this Response.

Sources report that several lawsuits were brought against the implementation of the Maisha card (Amnesty International 2024-09-16, 6; Nasubo 2024-12-05), related to concerns which include "data protection compliance, data security, [and] public participation as well as the inclusion of rural and marginalized communities" (Amnesty International 2024-09-16, 6). According to Keesing Platform, a news and resource website focused on biometrics, security printing, and identity documents published by Keesing Technologies (Keesing Technologies n.d.), a month after the Maisha card's launch in November 2023, the Kenya High Court paused the pilot program issuing it because a data protection assessment had not been completed (2024-02-27). Nasubo similarly notes that the High Court issued a ban on the government's implementation of the Maisha program on 5 December 2023, following a lawsuit "challenging the constitutionality of the Digital ID ecosystem" (2024-12-05).

According BiometricUpdate.com, a news website focused on the "global biometric market" published by the market research and consultancy company Biometrics Research Group (BiometricUpdate.com n.d.), a national ID card issuance "crisis" occurred when the rollout was stopped by the High Court at Nairobi, creating a backlog leaving "thousands" of applicants unable to obtain the ID cards (2024-02-07). According to Keesing Platform, 60,000 Kenyans applied before the injunction (2024-02-27). Sources indicate that the injunction was lifted on 23 February 2024 (Keesing Platform 2024-02-27) or 26 February 2024 (Nasubo 2024-12-05). According to Nasubo, another lawsuit led the court to block the issuance of the ID cards again on 26 July 2024; the order, which was lifted on 13 August 2024, affected "over" 10,000 individuals (2024-12-05).

Sources indicate there was a "public outcry" (The Kenya Times 2025-03-13) or "criticism" over the cost of [obtaining and (The Kenya Times 2025-03-13)] renewing the Maisha cards (The Kenya Times 2025-03-13; Professor 2025-08-26), since the previous versions were free or had a "nominal fee" (Professor 2025-08-26). According to the Kenya Times, the government planned an increase of fees for first-time applicants in 2023, but President William Ruto announced the "free issuance" of ID cards on 13 March 2025 (2025-03-13). The same source notes that in February 2025, Ruto had signed a decree to end "State discrimination" in the form of "extra vetting" of residents of North Kenya (The Kenya Times 2025-03-13). Further information on this decree and its implementation could not be found among the sources consulted by the Research Directorate within the time constraints of this Response.

4. Requirements and Procedures for Obtaining a National Identity Card in Kenya

4.1. Procedure for a First-Time Applicant

Information on the requirements and procedures to obtain the first-generation ID card could not be found among the sources consulted by the Research Directorate within the time constraints of this Response.

4.1.1. Second-Generation ID Card

For the second-generation national ID card, the Huduma Kenya Service Delivery Programme (HKSDP), a government-run program which provides services to citizens (Kenya n.d.c), states the following:

Application of initial IDs are done at the National Registration Bureau offices countrywide - usually located within County Commissioner's offices or Chiefs camp. At the National Registration Bureau office, you will fill a form detailing your bio data after which your finger prints and passport photograph shall be taken.

Requirements;

  • Above 18 years
  • Requirements for proof of age, names and place of birth;
  • Birth certificates
  • Religious [c]ertificates
  • School leaving [c]ertificates
  • Age assessment certificate from a medical officer of health.
  • Child health card
  • Notification of birth
  • Letter of administrative officer-Chief/Assistant Chief
  • Sworn [a]ffidavits for late registration
  • Proof in support of citizenship
  • Parent[s'] ID card for Kenyans by birth
  • Certificate of registration as a Kenyan citizen (Kenyan by registration or naturalization)

Persons who qualify for this service are Kenyan citizens who have never been issued with an Identity Card. A waiting card is issued to the applicant upon successful application and shall be surrendered when collecting the processed ID car[d.]

Cost: [100 Kenyan Shilling (KES) C$1.06]

Timelines: 10 days. (Kenya n.d.d)

4.1.2. Maisha Card and Digital ID

According to an article published by Aratek, a company that produces biometric devices and software for the global market (Aratek n.d.), first-time applicants can register for the Maisha card by visiting designated registration centres (2024-11-25). A document on the State Department for Immigration and Citizen Services website notes that as of 19 November 2024 there were registration offices in the following locations:

  • South Rift region (Narok, Kericho, Bomet, Nakuru, Laikipia, Baringo, and Samburu counties)
  • Nyanza region (Kisumu, Siaya, Homabay, Migori, Kisii, and Nyamira counties)
  • North Eastern region (Garissa, Wajir, and Mandera counties)
  • Central region (Nyeri, Nyandarua, Kirinyaga, Murang'a, and Kiambu counties)
  • Upper Eastern region (Embu, Meru, Tharaka Nithi, Marsabit, and Isiolo counties)
  • North Rift region (Uasin Gishu, Elgeyo Marakwet, Nandi, Trans-Nzoia, West Pokot, and Turkana counties)
  • Lower Eastern region (Makueni, Kitui, and Machakos counties)
  • Coast region (Mombasa, Kwale, Kilifi, Taita Taveta, Tana River, and Lamu counties)
  • Western region (Kakamega, Bungoma, Vihiga, and Busia counties)
  • Nairobi region (Nairobi and Kajiado counties) (Kenya 2024-11-19).

Further details on the specific locations of registration offices in 2024 are available in a document on the State Department for Immigration and Citizen Services website (Kenya 2024-11-19).

According to the State Department for Immigration and Citizen Services, a first-time applicant must provide proof of their name, age, and place of birth by providing any of the following documents:

  • Certificate of birth
  • Notification of birth
  • Child health card
  • Academic certificate
  • Age assessment certificate from a Medical Officer of Health
  • Letter from Administrative Officer-Chief/Assistant Chief
  • Religious certificate
  • Marriage certificate or parental consent in case of married applicant. (Kenya n.d.e)

Proof of citizenship is also required, for which any of the following documents may serve:

  • Either of the parents' identity card (Where parents are Kenyan by birth)
  • Parent's ID card and certificate of registration (Where [p]arent is Kenyan by registration)
  • Certificate of registration as a Kenyan citizen (Where the applicant is Kenyan by registration)
  • Any other document that may be required by the Registration Officer. (Kenya n.d.e)

In terms of the digital ID, the State Department for Immigration and Citizen Services states that applicants can register for the digital card via the eCitizen Gava Mkononi app (Kenya [2024], 18). The eCitizen website notes that the digital ID requires registering for an eCitizen account (Kenya n.d.b). The same source notes that each person is required to provide a scan of their face and fingerprints to activate the digital ID (Kenya n.d.b). If they are unable to complete these scans, they are directed to an "assisted activation" route to proceed (Kenya n.d.b).

4.2. Application for a Replacement Card

Sources note that a lost ID card should be reported at a police station and the police will provide the individual with a police abstract (Kenya n.d.f; The Kenya Times 2025-02-20), "which includes an [occurrence book] number and an official stamp confirming the report" (Kenya Times 2025-02-20). The Professor noted that a "reconfirmation of identity must occur" (2025-08-26).

Sources indicate that a replacement ID card can be obtained at any Huduma Centre [6] (The Kenya Times 2025-02-20; Kenya n.d.f) or registration office (Kenya n.d.f). Government sources indicate that there is a fee of 1,000 KES [C$10.70] (Kenya 1949, Sixth Schedule) or 1,050 Kes [C$11.40] (Kenya n.d.f). According to the State Department for Immigration and Citizen Services website, the fee is paid on the eCitizen app (Kenya n.d.f).

The information in the following paragraph was provided in an article by the Kenya Times:

The applicant must complete an application form and provide a police abstract and 2 passport-sized photographs. The photos can be taken at the Huduma Centre if the applicant does not have passport photos. The applicant's biometric details, including their fingerprints and photos, will be captured for verification purposes. A "waiting" card is issued and can serve as a temporary identification document while the new ID is being processed. The applicant will receive a text notification with a pick-up date when their new ID is ready (2025-02-20).

4.2.1. Requirements and Procedures for Obtaining or Replacing a National Identity Card Outside Kenya

The Kenya High Commission in Canada notes that all applicants must apply in person at the High Commission for biometrics capture (Kenya n.d.g).

The Kenya High Commission in Canada provides the following information regarding the requirements for first time applicants and holders of the first-generation ID cards (who are also considered first time applicants) to apply for a Kenyan ID card:

Form Reg. 136A duly completed.

1. Original 1st generation ID for holders of 1st generation IDs

2. Applicant's copy of Kenyan birth certificate

3. Three (3) passport size photographs recently taken on a white background.

4. Copy of applicant's Kenya[n] passport bio data page (page with the picture and information).

5. Copy of Kenyan [i]dentity card for parent/parents

6. A money order of CAD 70 payable to Kenya High Commission

…

OR

1. Original 1st generation ID for holders of 1st generation IDs

2. Three (3) passport size photographs recently taken on a white background.

3. Copy of applicant's Kenyan passport bio data page (page with the picture and information).

4. Copy of Registration certificate (for Kenyans by [r]egistration).

5. Residence permit/[n]aturalisation certificate/proof of having regained Kenya citizenship and or declared Kenya [c]itizenship. (Kenya n.d.g)

The Kenya High Commission in Canada provides the following information regarding the requirements to replace a Kenyan ID card:

Form Reg. 136A duly completed.

1. Police report or notarised affidavit explaining the circumstance of loss.

2. Two passport size photographs recently taken on a white background.

3. Copy of applicant's Kenyan passport bio data page

4. Copy of applicant's [i]dentity card

5. Copy of applicant's Kenya birth certificate.

6. Original and copy of ID (for duplicate and mutilated)

7. Copy of the lost ID.

8. A money order of CAD 70 payable to Kenya High Commission (Kenya n.d.g)

The Kenya High Commission in Canada provides the following information regarding the requirements to make changes to a Kenyan ID card:

Form Reg. 136A duly completed.

1. Applicant's original and copy of the ID.

2. Two passport size photographs recently taken on a white background.

3. Copy of applicant's Kenya[n] passport (bio data page).

4. Copy of marriage certificate (if foreign issued, to be notarised).

5. A copy of Dee[d] poll prepared and registered in Kenya accompanied by a copy of Kenya Gazette Notice effecting [n]ame change.

6. Ladies changing names as a result of marriage to be accompanied by their husbands at the time of submission of the application.

7. A money order of CAD 70 payable to Kenya High Commission (Kenya n.d.g)

The High Commission notes that all of the above applications should include a pre-paid self addressed return courier envelop (Kenya n.d.g).

The High Commission requires the completion of Form 136A, the same application form completed when applying for the National Identity Card within Kenya [see section 4.1.1. of this Response] (Kenya n.d.g).

5. Ability of State and Non-State Actors to Track Individuals Using the National Identity Card

Information on the ability to track individuals using the National Identity Card was scarce among the sources consulted by the Research Directorate within the time constraints of this Response.

The Data Protection Act, 2019 provides the following:

3. The object and purpose of this Act is—

a. to regulate the processing of personal data;

b. to ensure that the processing of personal data of a data subject is guided by the principles set out in section 25;

c. to protect the privacy of individuals;

d. to establish the legal and institutional mechanism to protect personal data; and

e. to provide data subjects with rights and remedies to protect their personal data from processing that is not in accordance with this Act. (Kenya 2019)

6. Fraudulent Cards

Information on fraudulent ID cards and genuine cards obtained through fraudulent means was scarce among the sources consulted by the Research Directorate within the time constraints of this Response.

6.1. Fraudulent First- and Second-Generation Cards

The law practitioner indicated that it is "[v]ery likely" that fraudulent ID cards are available (2025-09-19). However, according to the Professor, "fake or tampered" IDs are "comparatively rare" because national IDs are free and can be obtained with "minimal paperwork" (2025-08-26). The same source added that there is a "risk" of individuals using fraudulent first or second-generation ID cards for electoral fraud or fraudulent land sales, as these cards do not have "significant security features" (Professor 2025-08-26). The law practitioner similarly indicated that fraudulent IDs are "often" used in "land frauds" (2025-09-19).

The Professor stated that "poor quality" templates for fraudulent versions of the second-generation ID card are available online; however, "the algorithm for validating the 8-digit ID number with the 9-digit serial number is not publicly available or easily determined or hacked" (2025-08-26).

6.2. Fraudulent Maisha Cards

The Professor stated that due to the security features of the Maisha cards, which includes both the biometric chip and printing features, the card does not seem easy to replicate (2025-08-26).

According to Kenya Insights, an online Kenyan news source, a man was arrested for using 96 stolen national ID cards to register multiple "'Jiji accounts'" [a local classified website] to sell counterfeit telephones (2025-01-10).
