​​​​​​​Responses to Information Requests (RIRs) are research reports on country conditions. They are requested by IRB decision-makers.

The database contains a seven-year archive of English and French RIRs. Earlier RIRs may be found on the European Country of Origin Information Network website.

RIR​s published by the IRB on its website may have attachments that are inaccessible due to technical constraints and may include translations of documents originally written in languages other than English or French. To obtain a copy of such attachments and/or translated version of the RIR attachments, please email us.​

Responses to Information Requests (RIRs) cite publicly accessible information available at the time of publication and within time constraints. A list of references and additional sources consulted are included in each RIR. Sources cited are considered the most current information available as of the date of the RIR.            

RIRs are not, and do not purport to be, conclusive as to the merit of any particular claim for refugee protection. Rather, they are intended to support the refugee determination process. More information on the methodology used by the Research Directorate can be found here.          

The assessment and weight to be given to the information in the RIRs are the responsibility of independent IRB members (decision-makers) after considering the evidence and arguments presented by the parties.           

The information presented in RIRs solely reflects the views and perspectives of the sources cited and does not necessarily reflect the position of the IRB or the Government of Canada.          

21 May 2026

BGD202591.E

Bangladesh: The Special Powers Act, including implementation by authorities; police procedures directed by the Act, including warrants and notifications (2024–April 2026)

Research Directorate, Immigration and Refugee Board of Canada

1. The Special Powers Act, 1974

Bangladesh's Special Powers Act describes itself as "[a]n Act to provide for special measures for the prevention of certain prejudicial activities, for more speedy trial and effective punishment of certain grave offences and for matters connected therewith" (Bangladesh 1974, preamble, emphasis omitted). Section 2(f) of the Act defines prejudicial activities as follows:

(f) "prejudicial act" means any act which is intended or likely-

(i) to prejudice the sovereignty or defence of Bangladesh;

(ii) to prejudice the maintenance of friendly relations of Bangladesh with foreign states;

(iii) to prejudice the security of Bangladesh or to endanger public safety or the maintenance of public order;

(iv) to create or excite feelings of enmity or hatred between different communities, classes or sections of people;

(v) to interfere with or encourage or incite interference with the administration of law or the maintenance of law and order;

(vi) to prejudice the maintenance of supplies and services essential to the community;

(vii) to cause fear or alarm to the public or to any section of the public;

(viii) to prejudice the economic or financial interests of the State… . (Bangladesh 1974)

Section 3(1) of the Special Powers Act provides the following:

Power to make orders detaining or removing certain persons

3. (1) The Government may, if satisfied with respect to any person that with a view to preventing him from doing any prejudicial act it is necessary so to do, make an order-

(a) directing that such person be detained;

(b) directing him to remove himself from Bangladesh in such manner, before such time and by such route as may be specified in the order:

Provided that no order of removal shall be made in respect of any citizen of Bangladesh. (Bangladesh 1974)

Human Rights Watch states that the Special Powers Act allows for "preventive detention" (2025-07-30). Similarly, according to the US Department of State's Country Reports on Human Rights Practices for 2024, the Act grants "legal justification for arrests that would often otherwise be considered arbitrary," as it "remove[s] the requirement that arrests be based on crimes" that have already occurred (US 2024-04-22, 12).

2. Implementation of the Special Powers Act by Authorities

An article published by the Bangladesh chapter of the Network for International Law Students (NILS) [1], written by an "apprentice lawyer" and an advocate, indicates that section 3 of the Special Powers Act allows the government to order "preventive detention" based on the suspicion that an individual may commit a "credible prejudicial act" (NILS Bangladesh 2024-12-17). An opinion article by Noshin Nawal, a barrister who is also an "activist, feminist and a columnist" for the Bangladeshi newspaper the Daily Star, observes that "vague" terms such as "'prejudicial to national security'" and "'disturbing public order'" allow authorities to target "virtually anyone," including journalists, opposition figures and students (Nawal 2025-03-05).

2.1. Under the Hasina Government

The Asian Human Rights Commission (AHRC), an independent NGO which promotes human rights in Asia (AHRC n.d.), indicates in 2022 that "[j]ailing dissidents" under the Special Powers Act and other "draconian" laws, is the "norm" in Bangladesh (2022-07-29). In correspondence with the Research Directorate, a human rights advocate, who is also a former member of the Commission of Inquiry on Enforced Disappearances [2], indicated that "the practice that developed" under the Sheikh Hasina government was to register criminal cases, "typically against political opponents," for "destabilizing public safety and security" under section 15(3) of the Special Powers Act; this section, unlike other sections of the Act, operates "within the ordinary criminal trial framework," meaning that the accused can apply for bail and is protected from "arbitrary preventive detention" (Human rights advocate 2026-04-27). However, the same source adds that section 15 of the Act [3] defines "'prejudicial acts' broadly" to include a "wide range of political, public or civil society activity" and does not require "specific evidence of a criminal act" (Human rights advocate 2026-04-27).

Odhikar, a human rights organization in Bangladesh that documents rights violations (Odhikar n.d.), reports that in 2023, police arrested a 60-year-old woman under the Special Powers Act after her US-based son criticized the government on Facebook; she was released on bail after 8 days in jail (2024-01-04, 22, 47). Amnesty International reports that, following alleged bank robberies and a hostage-taking by an armed group in April 2024 in the Chittagong Hill Tracts, Bangladesh authorities arrested 142 Indigenous Bawm people accused of "sedition under Section 15" of the Act, as well as "other offences under the Penal Code," without "establish[ing] individual criminal responsibility" through evidence (2025-12-12). The same source adds that, as of December 2025, 59 of these individuals remained in detention without trial (Amnesty International 2025-12-12).

2.2. Under the Interim Government [4]

Human Rights Watch reports that under the interim government, hundreds of individuals "may have been arrested" under the Special Powers Act (2025-07-30). According to the newspaper Dhaka Tribune [5], out of approximately 22,000 cases [complaints] filed with police nationwide between the fall of the Hasina government on 5 August 2024 and the national election on 12 February 2026, 1,200 cases were filed under the Special Powers Act and the Digital Security Act [6] (2026-02-27). The human rights advocate indicated that since the fall of the Hasina government, the "overwhelming majority" of cases have been filed under the Penal Code (2026-04-27). For further information on criminal cases registered during the interim government's administration, see Response to Information Request BGD202592 of May 2026.

Sources indicate that on 9 April 2025, a model and winner of a Miss Earth Bangladesh pageant was arrested, and a court ordered her to be detained for 30 days under the Special Powers Act (The Daily Star 2025-04-13; The Financial Express 2025-04-29). The Daily Star notes that the arrest was made on charges of "spreading lies about an important individual to deteriorate relations between countries" (The Daily Star 2025-04-13). The Financial Express, a Bangladesh-based newspaper, indicates that the model was released from prison after being granted bail on 29 April 2025 (The Financial Express 2025-04-29).

2.3. Under the Bangladesh Nationalist Party (BNP) [7] Government Elected in February 2026

The Bangladesh Sangbad Sangstha (BSS), a "state-run news agency" (bdnews24.com 2026-04-07), quotes the Law, Justice, and Parliamentary Affairs Minister of Bangladesh as stating that to prevent fuel shortages, the BNP government will use the Special Powers Act against those who hoard fuel and sell it on the "'black marke[t]'," including the "'maximum penalty of death'" (2026-04-04). Another BSS article notes that 4 people were arrested for hoarding and reselling fuel at higher prices in April 2026; their cases are registered under the Special Powers Act (2026-04-18). The Dhaka Tribune notes that 2 men were arrested for possession of heroin and counterfeit money and were charged under both the Special Powers Act and the Narcotics Control Act (2026-04-12).

3. Procedures for Detention Under the Act

Sections 3(2) to 3(5) of the Special Powers Act provide the following:

(2) Any District Magistrate or Additional District Magistrate may, if satisfied with respect to any person that with a view to preventing him from doing any prejudicial act within the meaning of section 2(f) (iii), (iv), (v), (vi), (vii) or (viii) it is necessary so to do, make an order directing that such person be detained.

(3) When any order is made under sub-section (2), the District Magistrate or the Additional District Magistrate making the order shall forthwith report the fact to the Government together with the grounds on which the order has been made and such other particulars as, in his opinion, have a bearing on the matter, and no such order shall remain in force for more than thirty days after the making thereof unless in the meantime it has been approved by the Government.

(4) If any person fails to remove himself from Bangladesh in accordance with the direction of an order made under sub-section (1) (b) [8], then, without prejudice to the provisions of sub-section (5), he may be so removed by any police officer or by any person authorised by the Government in this behalf.

(5) If any person contravenes any order made under sub-section (1) (b), he shall be punishable with imprisonment for a term which may extend to three years, or with fine, or with both. (Bangladesh 1974, bold in original)

The human rights advocate stated that

[t]he Special Powers Act contains no prescribed police procedural framework of the kind ordinarily required by criminal law. Under standard Bangladeshi criminal procedure (the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1898), a police officer must either obtain a judicial warrant or witness the commission of a cognizable offence before effecting an arrest. The Act displaces this framework. …

Section 3 vests the detention power in the government or a district magistrate — an executive, not a judicial, authority. The detention order itself constitutes the entire legal authorization. No court issues it, no judge reviews it at the point of arrest, and no judicial officer is notified. (2026-04-27)

Section 6 of the Special Powers Act provides the following:

6. No detention order shall be invalid or inoperative merely by reason that the person to be detained thereunder is outside the limits of the territorial jurisdiction of the Government or the District Magistrate or Additional District Magistrate making the order, or that the place of detention of such person is outside the said limits. (Bangladesh 1974)

The human rights advocate stated that section 6 of the Act "permits detention for up to 30 days before any form of review is required" (2026-04-27). Sources note that the Act authorizes the detention of individuals "without" trial (Daily Observer 2025-04-11; Foysal 2025-04-25) for a "specified" duration, "primarily" to maintain "public safety, national security, and law and order" (Daily Observer 2025-04-11).

The Special Powers Act provides the following:

Reference to Advisory Board

9. (1) The Government shall, whenever necessary, constitute an Advisory Board for the purposes of this Act.

…

10. In every case where a detention order has been made under this Act, the Government shall, within one hundred and twenty days from the date of detention under the order, place before the Advisory Board constituted under section 9 the grounds on which the order has been made and the representation, if any, made by the person affected by the order.

…

Action upon the report of Advisory Board

12. (1) In any case where the Advisory Board has reported that there is, in its opinion, sufficient cause for the detention of a person, the Government may confirm the detention order and continue the detention of the person concerned for such period as it thinks fit:

Provided that the Advisory Board shall, after affording the person concerned an opportunity of being heard in person, review such detention order, unless revoked earlier, once in every six months from the date of such detention order and the Government shall inform the person concerned of the result of such review.

(2) In any case where the Advisory Board has reported that there is, in its opinion, no sufficient cause for the detention of the person concerned, the Government shall revoke the detention order and cause the person to be released forthwith.

Revocation of detention orders

13. A detention order may, at any time, be revoked or modified by the Government.

Temporary release of persons detained

14. (1) The Government may, at any time, direct that any person detained in pursuance of a detention order may be released for any specified period either without conditions or upon such conditions specified in the direction as that person accepts, and may, at any time, cancel his release.

… (Bangladesh 1974)

The human rights advocate stated that under sections 9 to 14 of the Act, the government appoints an advisory board to review detentions that are extended beyond the initial 30 days; the board is not "an independent judicial body" (Human rights advocate 2026-04-27). Nawal notes that the initial detention is set for 6 months, but "can be renewed indefinitely" (2025-03-05).

The Special Powers Act provides the following:

Procedure of Special Tribunals

27. (1) Notwithstanding anything contained in the Code or in any other law for the time being in force, a Special Tribunal may take cognizance of an offence triable under this Act without the accused being committed to it for trial, but shall not take cognizance of any such offence except on a report in writing made by a police officer not below the rank of Sub-Inspector.

…

Appeals and confirmation of death sentences

…

(2) Where a Special Tribunal passes a sentence of death, the proceedings shall be submitted forthwith to the High Court Division and the sentence shall not be executed unless it is confirmed by that Division.]

…

Execution of death sentences

[34A. When a person is sentenced to death under this Act, the sentence may be executed by hanging him by the neck till he is dead or by shooting him in the prescribed manner till he is dead as the Special Tribunal may direct. (Bangladesh 1974, emphasis and brackets in original, footnote omitted)

The information in the following paragraph was provided by the human rights advocate:

Traditionally, the courts have "shown reluctance to interfere with executive detentions" that are "framed as matters of public safety or national security." The Act has "no independent audit mechanisms"; the oversight structures are "controlled by the same executive authority" issuing the detention order or are "structurally constrained in ways that limit their practical effect." When the Act is used, there are "no statutory mechanisms" for complaints against the police and no access to detention records by civil society. Moreover, individual detentions under the Act are not subject to "parliamentary oversight" and it is not "mandatory" to report such detentions to any public body (Human rights advocate 2026-04-27). Corroborating information could not be found among the sources consulted by the Research Directorate within the time constraints of this Response.

3.1. Use of Warrants and Notifications

Section 4 of the Special Powers Act provides the following:

Execution of detention orders

4. A detention order may be executed at any place in Bangladesh in the manner provided for the execution of warrants of arrest under the Code. (Bangladesh 1974, bold in original)

US Country Reports 2024 states that while "arbitrary arrest and detention" is prohibited under Bangladesh's constitution, arrest and detention is permitted by law "without an order from a magistrate or a warrant" if the authorities decide an individual may be a "threat to security and public order" or is suspected of a "serious crime" (US 2024-04-22, 20). Sources indicate that the Special Powers Act authorizes the police to arrest individuals without a warrant (RSF 2023-08-23; human rights advocate 2026-04-27). However, the human rights advocate added that the Act allows "warrant-based action in certain circumstances," and warrants are "occasionally" secured, particularly when the investigator is unsure of the "evidentiary foundation" or "legal basis for detention"; warrant-less detentions are "more likely" when "executive confidence is high," including in cases with a "political dimension" (2026-04-27).

In an article on the case of Miss Earth Bangladesh, the newspaper Daily Observer [9] notes that she livestreamed to Facebook while the police "forcibly entered" her home; the Dhaka Metropolitan Police, however, "denied [the] allegation" that the model was "abducted," stating that she was "taken into custody following all legal procedures" as a result of "national security concerns" (Daily Observer 2025-04-11). Other sources note that the Bangladesh High Court questioned the legality of the model's detention (The Daily Star 2025-04-13) or directed the authorities to explain her detention, citing issues such as "the lack of a warrant, delayed court appearance, and denial of legal aid" (HRRC 2025-04-15).

The Special Powers Act provides the following:

Communications of grounds of order.

8.(1) In every case where an order has been made under section 3, the authority making the order shall, as soon as may be, but subject to the provisions of sub-section (2), communicate to the person affected thereby the grounds on which the order has been made to enable him to make a representation in writing against the order, and it shall be the duty of such authority to inform such person of his right of making such representation and to afford him the earliest opportunity of doing so:

Provided that nothing in this section shall require the authority to disclose the facts which it considers to be against the public interest to disclose.

(2) In the case of a detention order, the authority making the order shall inform the person detained under that order of the grounds of his detention at the time he is detained or as soon thereafter as is practicable, but not later than fifteen days from the date of detention. (Bangladesh 1974, emphasis in original)

The human rights advocate stated that the Act directs authorities to communicate the reason for detention to the detainee "'as soon as may be'," which is "undefined" and has "no enforceable deadline"; it also allows the authorities to withhold from the detainee information considered to be "against the public interest to disclose" (2026-04-27). Other sources note that under this Act, detainees are held without being informed of the reason for their detention (Nawal 2025-03-05; Foysal 2025-04-25). The human rights advocate further stated that this Act does not require notification of detention to be provided to "family members, legal counsel, any court or judicial officer, any independent oversight body, consular authorities, or any public register" (2026-04-27).

Nawal indicates that the detainees are imprisoned without access to legal counsel (2025-03-05). The human rights advocate similarly noted that detainees do not have the right to legal counsel before the government-appointed advisory board; moreover, they cannot "examine or challenge" the evidence, although they can make a "written representation" (2026-04-27). The same source added that the board's decisions are not subject to "judicial review on the merits" (Human rights advocate 2026-04-27).
