Responses to Information Requests

​​​​​​​Responses to Information Requests (RIRs) are research reports on country conditions. They are requested by IRB decision-makers.

The database contains a seven-year archive of English and French RIRs. Earlier RIRs may be found on the European Country of Origin Information Network website.

RIR​s published by the IRB on its website may have attachments that are inaccessible due to technical constraints and may include translations of documents originally written in languages other than English or French. To obtain a copy of such attachments and/or translated version of the RIR attachments, please email us.​

Disclaimer

Responses to Information Requests (RIRs) cite publicly accessible information available at the time of publication and within time constraints. A list of references and additional sources consulted are included in each RIR. Sources cited are considered the most current information available as of the date of the RIR.            

RIRs are not, and do not purport to be, conclusive as to the merit of any particular claim for refugee protection. Rather, they are intended to support the refugee determination process. More information on the methodology used by the Research Directorate can be found here.          

The assessment and weight to be given to the information in the RIRs are the responsibility of independent IRB members (decision-makers) after considering the evidence and arguments presented by the parties.           

The information presented in RIRs solely reflects the views and perspectives of the sources cited and does not necessarily reflect the position of the IRB or the Government of Canada.

Mexico: The socio-economic situation, including demographics, employment rates, economic sectors, and crime rates, particularly in Mexico City, Mérida, Puebla, Campeche, and Monterrey (2020–September 2023)

Research Directorate, Immigration and Refugee Board of Canada

1. Cost of Living

The same source lists changes in the price index between January 2023 and June 2023 by category of home, as follows:

  • Single-detached house, 11.8 percent increase;
  • Condominium and apartment, 11.4 percent increase;
  • Low-cost and social housing, 11.1 percent increase; and
  • Middle-income and residential housing [2], 11.9 percent increase (Mexico 2023-08-10, 2).

2. Mexico City (National Capital)

According to a report on the results of a 2020 population and housing census by INEGI for Mexico City, the national capital's population is 9,209,944, with 4,805,017 women and 4,404,927 men (Mexico 2021-01-25a, 3-4). In a sociodemographic overview of Mexico City using the results of the 2020 population and housing census, INEGI indicates that the population density of Mexico City is 6,163.3 inhabitants per square kilometre (Mexico 2021a, 8). The 2020 census report for Mexico City indicates that the median age is 35 (Mexico 2021-01-25a, 9). According to the sociodemographic overview of Mexico City, the marital status breakdown for the population is as follows:

  • Married, 29.9 percent;
  • Single, 38.1 percent;
  • Common-law, 17.2 percent;
  • Separated, 6.1 percent;
  • Divorced, 3.1 percent; and
  • Widowed, 5.4 percent (Mexico 2021a, 9).

According to the 2020 census report for Mexico City states that 9.2 percent of the population aged 3 years and above [translation] "self-identifies as Indigenous" (Mexico 2021-01-25a, 44). The sociodemographic overview of Mexico City indicates that 2.05 percent of the population [translation] "self-identifies as Afro-Mexican, Black, or Afro-descendant" (Mexico 2021a, 8). Citing results from the 2020 census, DataMéxico states that 1.42 percent of the population aged 15 and over in Mexico City is illiterate; among this group, 31.3 percent are men and 68.7 percent are women (Mexico n.d.d).

2.1 Employment and Economic Sectors

The 2020 census report for Mexico City states that 74.3 percent of men and 55.6 percent of women aged 12 and over are in the labour force, a total of 64.4 percent of the population over the age of 12 (Mexico 2021-01-25a, 79). INEGI's sociodemographic overview of Mexico City indicates that 97.8 percent of the labour force is employed: 97.4 percent of men and 98.2 percent of women (Mexico 2021a, 9). The same source indicates that 35.4 percent of the population aged 12 and over is not active in the economy and gives the following breakdown of that group:

  • Students, 37.1 percent;
  • Homemakers, 34.3 percent;
  • Pensioners or retirees, 16.8 percent;
  • People with a physical or mental disability that prevents them from working, 2.8 percent; and
  • People [translation] "in other non-economic activities," 9.0 percent (Mexico 2021a, 9).

The information on Mexico City in the following paragraph was provided by INEGI in its national employment survey report for the first quarter of 2023:

The unemployment rate of the population aged 15 years and older was 3.7 percent. The informal employment rate [5] was 49 percent. The employment rate in the informal sector [6] was 32.5 percent. The [translation] "main" economic sector was retail, which accounted for 19.9 percent of the city's economic activity (Mexico 2023-05, 3, 16, 22, 24, 31).

The 2020 census report for Mexico City indicates that 0.6 percent of jobholders work in the agricultural sector, 13.3 percent work in the industrial and construction sector, and 82 percent work in the trade and services sector (Mexico 2021-01-25a, 81). The same source indicates that the working population breaks down as follows:

  • 71 percent are salaried workers
  • 3.4 percent are employers
  • 24.2 percent are self-employed
  • 1.3 percent are workers without pay (Mexico 2021-01-25a, 82).

2.3 Housing

The 2020 census report for Mexico City states that 55.6 percent of dwellings in the city are owned by the occupants, 24.5 percent are rented, and 19.9 percent are [translation] "borrowed or in another situation" (Mexico 2021-01-25a, 144). The Federal Mortgage Corporation reports that its housing price index for Mexico City increased by 13.1 percent in the first half of 2023 compared to the same period in 2022 (Mexico 2023-08-10, 4). Interviewed for a New York Times article on Mexico City real estate, the general director of ForHouse, "an agency that specializes in working with foreign buyers," stated that "finding rentals is very difficult – there are not a lot of apartments available" (2022-11-16). Additionally, the New York Times article reports that local residents of Mexico City are experiencing an increase in rental prices and "changing neighborhoods" due to the "rise of Airbnb and a tighter market overall" (2022-11-16). Corroborating information could not be found among the sources consulted by the Research Directorate within the time constraints of this Response.

6.1 Employment and Economic Sectors

INEGI's sociodemographic overview for Nuevo León State, citing results from the 2020 census, indicates that 60 percent of the population aged 12 and above in Monterrey is [translation] "economically active"; 59.1 percent of the labour force consists of men and 40.9 percent of women (Mexico 2021e, 95). According to the same source, 98.1 percent of economically active individuals are employed, 97.7 percent of men and 98.7 percent of women (Mexico 2021e, 95).

The same source indicates that 39.4 percent of the population aged 12 and over in Monterrey is not active in the economy and gives the following breakdown of that group:

  • Students, 32 percent;
  • Homemakers, 37.9 percent;
  • Pensioners or retirees, 19.4 percent;
  • People with a physical or mental disability that prevents them from working, 2.3 percent; and
  • People [translation] "in other non-economic activities," 8.3 percent (Mexico 2021e, 95).

The information on Monterrey in the following paragraph was provided by INEGI in its national employment survey report for the first quarter of 2023:

The unemployment rate of the population age 15 years old and older is 3.2 percent. The informal employment rate was 34.2 percent. The employment rate in the informal sector was 20.6 percent. Notes

[1] According to an article by Francisco José Zamudio Sánchez et al. published in INEGI's Reality, Data and Space International Journal of Statistics and Geography, the housing index maintained by Mexico's Federal Mortgage Corporation (Sociedad Hipotecaria Federal, SHF) only includes houses sold with mortgages (Zamudio Sánchez, et al. 2020-08, 41).

[2] Professor Aurora Poó Rubio, in an article on housing in Mexico published by the Universidad Autónoma Metropolitana-Azcapotzalco, explains that the housing industry is divided into categories by [translation] "price and source of financing," and categories include middle-income (vivienda media or de tipo medio) and residential (residencial) (Poó Rubio 2007, 65). Middle-income housing [translation] "generally includes houses financed by banks for first- and second-time homebuyers," while "residential" houses "are generally financed by commercial banks for second- and third-time homebuyers" (Poó Rubio 2007, 65).

[3] Mexico's National Institute of Statistics and Geography (Instituto Nacional de Estadística y Geografía, INEGI) is the "autonomous public body" responsible for gathering and sharing information on Mexico's population, economy, territory and resources (Mexico n.d.a).

[4] DataMéxico is an online platform which presents data from a number of databases in a visual way and is administered by Mexico's Ministry of Economy (Mexico n.d.b).

[5] INEGI's May 2023 national survey on occupation and employment defines the informal employment rate as the [translation] "percentage of the working population that is occupationally vulnerable due to the nature of the economic unit for which they work and/or whose labour relationship or dependency does not give them access to social security or is not recognized by their source of work" (Mexico 2023-05, 32).

[6] INEGI defines the [translation] "rate of employment in the informal sector" as the percentage of the working population that works for a home-based economic entity that has not established itself as a company and is not identifiable independent of the household (Mexico 2023-05, 32).

[7] DataMéxico published raw data on labour force distribution by occupation from INEGI's 2023 employment survey in Excel format. The percentages provided in this Response to Information Request are based on calculations made by the Research Directorate using Excel.

[8] DataMéxico published raw data on criminal complaints from Mexico's Executive Secretariat of the National Public Security System (Secretariado Ejecutivo del Sistema Nacional de Seguridad Pública, SESNSP) in Excel format. [9] The Institute for Economics and Peace (IEP) is an international think tank based in Australia that researches the economic impact of violence, producing reports on the topic (IEP n.d.).
