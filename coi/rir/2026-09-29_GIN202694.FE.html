​​​​​​​Responses to Information Requests (RIRs) are research reports on country conditions. They are requested by IRB decision-makers.

The database contains a seven-year archive of English and French RIRs. Earlier RIRs may be found on the European Country of Origin Information Network website.

RIR​s published by the IRB on its website may have attachments that are inaccessible due to technical constraints and may include translations of documents originally written in languages other than English or French. To obtain a copy of such attachments and/or translated version of the RIR attachments, please email us.​

Disclaimer

Responses to Information Requests (RIRs) cite publicly accessible information available at the time of publication and within time constraints. A list of references and additional sources consulted are included in each RIR. Sources cited are considered the most current information available as of the date of the RIR.            

RIRs are not, and do not purport to be, conclusive as to the merit of any particular claim for refugee protection. Rather, they are intended to support the refugee determination process. More information on the methodology used by the Research Directorate can be found here.          

The assessment and weight to be given to the information in the RIRs are the responsibility of independent IRB members (decision-makers) after considering the evidence and arguments presented by the parties.           

The information presented in RIRs solely reflects the views and perspectives of the sources cited and does not necessarily reflect the position of the IRB or the Government of Canada.          

29 September 2026

GIN202694.FE

Guinea: Situation and treatment of children born out of wedlock by society and authorities; state protection and support services available (2019−September 2026)

Research Directorate, Immigration and Refugee Board of Canada

1. Prevalence

In a January 2023 report on children born out of wedlock, France's Office for the Protection of Refugees and Stateless Persons (Office français de protection des réfugiés et apatrides, OFPRA) states that, according to [translation] "several sources" consulted, pregnancies out of wedlock remain "a common occurrence" in Guinea (France 2023-01-20, 3). Recent statistics on the prevalence of pregnancies out of wedlock could not be found among the sources consulted by the Research Directorate within the time constraints of this Response.

According to the Guinean Organization for the Defence of Human and Citizen Rights (Organisation guinéenne de défense des droits de l'homme et du citoyen, OGDH) and a community development NGO interviewed by Belgium's Office of the Commissioner General for Refugees and Stateless Persons (CGRS) in 2019, pregnancies out of wedlock, or the desire to avoid them, are among the leading causes of forced marriages in Guinea (Belgium 2020-12-15, 7). According to the 2018 Guinea Demographic and Health Survey [1], 46.5% of women aged 20 to 24 were in their first union (i.e., had [translation] "started living with [their] first husband [or] partner") before the age of 18 (INS of Guinea & ICF 2019-07, 78) and 38.9% of women aged 20 to 24 had given birth before the age of 18 (INS of Guinea & ICF 2019-07, 98).

2. Societal Perception

OFPRA indicates, in the summary of its 2023 report on children born out of wedlock, that pregnancies out of wedlock remain [translation] "a disgrace for mothers and their families in a country where marriage remains a strong institution" (France 2023-01-20, 2). The same source explains that, according to [translation] "several sources" consulted, "the birth of a child resulting from sexual relations outside of marriage is considered a matter of dishonour or humiliation" (France 2023-01-20, 4, 5). In correspondence with the Research Directorate, the Association of Guinean Women Journalists (Association des femmes journalistes de Guinée, AFJ-G) similarly stated that having [translation] "a child out of wedlock is seen as a family dishonour" (AFJ-G 2026-08-12). A 2024 article from the online news website Guineematin.com likewise reports that [translation] "[i]n Guinean society, having children out of wedlock is viewed very negatively because of social and religious pressures" (2024-11-20).

A 2023 report published by the World Bank notes that religious beliefs and traditional norms "prohibit" sexual intercourse out of wedlock (de Paz, et al. 2023-05-10, 6). According to a Christian religious leader interviewed in 2024 by the author of the Guineematin.com article, in the eyes of the church, couples who conceive a child out of wedlock should [translation] "'redeem'" themselves and "'normalize'" things by "regular[izing] their marital status" through a religious marriage (2024-11-20). An imam and columnist consulted for another Guineematin.com article stated that women who have had children out of wedlock [translation] "have made a great mistake" and must "repent" before they can marry (2023-01-25).

In correspondence sent to the Research Directorate, the President of Action for the Health of Mothers and Children in Guinea (Actions pour la santé des mères et des enfants de Guinée, ASMEG), a Guinean non-political and humanitarian NGO that promotes maternal and child health (OIF n.d.), noted that when a child born out of wedlock is the result of adultery, [translation] "the situation is often perceived as even more serious" and that adultery is considered "a crime against the honour of the family and the betrayed husband" (ASMEG 2026-08-09). In a 2020 report by Guinéenews on children born out of wedlock in the Foutah Djallon region, a [translation] "very religious region" of Guinea, a Quranic teacher stated that fornication and adultery were among the "'greatest sins in Islam'" and that they were "'practices condemned and punished by the Muslim religion'" (2020-11-17).

Articles published in Guinean news sources report examples of suicide or attempted suicide, by pregnant individuals or their family members, related to pregnancy out of wedlock (Guinéenews 2023-01-04; Guineematin.com 2021-10-19).

For information on the situation and treatment of single women and female heads of households, see Response to Information Request GIN202692 of September 2026.

3. Treatment by Society

Guineematin.com reports that children born out of wedlock in Guinea are considered a [translation] "disgrace" (2021-06-10). According to ASMEG's president, children born of adultery are, for their part, perceived as the [translation] "quintessential 'children of sin'" (2026-08-09).

Sources report that children born out of wedlock are [translation] "stigmatized," whether within their families (ASMEG 2026-0809) or by society in general (Guineematin.com 2024-11-20). According to the Christian religious leader interviewed for the Guineematin.com article, while the church [translation] "welcomes" all children born out of wedlock, those simply born out of wedlock may avoid this stigma if their parents later marry; however, children born of an adulterous relationship are "'sometimes'" or "'often'" stigmatized (2024-11-20).

A sociologist quoted in the Guinéenews article about children born out of wedlock in the Foutah Djallon region noted that these children [translation] "'are reminded that they are different from others'" (2020-11-17). News sources report that these children are referred to as [translation] "'bastards'" (Guinéenews 2020-11-17; Podcast Journal 2019-09-11).

Sources also report that children born outside of marriage are [translation] "rejected" by society (AFJ-G 2026-08-12; Guinéenews 2020-11-17) and that it is "'difficult, if not impossible'" for them to find someone who will agree to marry them (Guinéenews 2020-11-17). According to ASMEG's president, [translation] "families may oppose the marriage of their children to a person born out of wedlock, for fear of 'sullying' their own lineage" (2026-08-09).

The author of the Guineematin.com article also noted that [translation] "[s]ome" parents of these children born out of wedlock deprive them of inheritance (2024-11-20). According to ASMEG's president, [translation] "although Guinean law has evolved, in customary practice, a child born out of wedlock may lose out in inheritance matters or even be excluded from inheritance. Recognition of the child's filiation is often more difficult, which may affect the child's inheritance rights" (2026-08-09). See section 4.1 of this Response for information on legislation in Guinea.

Finally, according to ASMEG's president, children born out of wedlock can be targets of violence and abuse, including from their own families or from the community (2026-08-09). A child born out of wedlock may be [translation] "deprived of the affection and support normally given to the other children in the family" and may "see their access to health care, adequate food or schooling compromised" (ASMEG 2026-08-09). The same source stated the following:

[translation]

Children born out of wedlock are particularly vulnerable to physical and psychological violence, often perpetrated by the maternal or paternal family. Cases of abandonment and infanticide have been reported. … Simply by being born, [these children] are exposed to inhuman and degrading treatment. (ASMEG 2026-08-09)

Guinean news articles similarly report the abandonment (Guinee7.com 2026-08-06; Guinéenews 2020-11-17; Guineematin.com 2021-06-10) and infanticide of children born out of wedlock (Guinee7.com 2026-08-06; Guinéenews 2020-11-17).

According to ASMEG's president, being the product of adultery can expose a child born out of wedlock to even harsher treatment: [translation] "The child becomes the living symbol of [the mother's] 'betrayal' and bears the brunt of the anger and rejection of the wronged husband and his family" (2026-08-09). Moreover,

[translation]

[in cases of adultery,] the parent-child relationship ties are denied more vigorously. The husband's family may categorically refuse to recognize the child, leaving them without any family protection. The pressure on the mother to abandon the child is immense and it often leads to marital breakdown and complete rejection of the mother and her child. (ASMEG 2026-08-09)

According to ASMEG's president, the stigmatization of children born out of wedlock persists into adulthood, [translation] "although it can take more subtle forms" (2026-08-09). The same source reported that [translation] "[t]he adult can be the target of gossip, contempt and social ostracism. Their 'illegitimate' birth is sometimes used against them in community or professional disputes" (ASMEG 2026-08-09).

3.1. Treatment Depending on Context

The 2023 OFPRA report highlights the following, without specifying its sources, regarding the factors that may influence the treatment of children born out of wedlock:

[translation]

It appears that the situation of a child born out of wedlock varies considerably according to the social setting, the mother's financial situation and the family's lifestyle. Thus, the more modern and urban the setting, the greater the possibility of living with dignity despite being a child born out of wedlock. (France 2023-01-20, 5)

ASMEG's president indicated the following in 2026:

[translation]

In rural areas, where social cohesion is stronger and customary rules are more strict, the stigma is often greater, more visible and harder to escape. The consequences in terms of ostracism may be more immediate. In urban centres such as Conakry, the relative anonymity and diversity of lifestyles may offer some protection, but stigmatization remains prevalent within families. (2026-08-09)

3.2. Treatment Depending on Ethnic Group

Information on the variations in treatment of children born out of wedlock based on their ethnic group was scarce among the sources consulted by the Research Directorate within the time constraints of this Response.

The OFPRA report indicates that, according to information in a 2013 report based on CGRS data, [translation] "ostracism towards children born out of wedlock is strongest in the Peul [2] community" (France 2023-01-20, 5). Corroborating information could not be found among the sources consulted by the Research Directorate within the time constraints of this Response.

According to an article by Podcast Journal, an international news outlet that also publishes podcast content (Podcast Journal 2022-05-22),

[translation]

among the Soussou [Susu] and the Forest [3] groups, sex is less taboo and cohabitation rules are less rigorous. Partners generally spend many years living together or being engaged and have children before getting married. As a result, early pregnancies out of wedlock are common. (2019-09-11)

ASMEG's president stated the following:

[translation]

The importance of traditions varies across ethnic groups. Attachment to lineage and patrilineal filiation is a key factor. The shame associated with a pregnancy out of wedlock is a shared concept, but the consequences (such as exclusion from succession) may be more formalized in some groups than in others. (2026-08-09)

3.3. Treatment Depending on Religion

Information on the influence of religion on the treatment of children born out of wedlock was scarce among the sources consulted by the Research Directorate within the time constraints of this Response.

The Guinéenews report on births out of wedlock in the [translation] "deeply religious" Muslim region of Foutah Djallon notes that, in addition to tradition, which opposes "intimate relations outside the sacred bond of marriage[,] ... there is also the influence of the Muslim religion, which is uncompromising on this issue. Islam does not permit any kind of relations outside marriage" (2020-11-17).

The Christian religious leader cited in the Guineematin.com article explained that the church [translation] "welcomes" children born out of wedlock but expects their parents to marry in order to "'normalize'" their situation (2024-11-20).

According to ASMEG's president, [translation] "religion, whether Muslim or Christian, plays a major role in reinforcing marriage as a social norm. A child born out of wedlock is seen as a transgression of religious precepts, which contributes to moral condemnation and exclusion" (2026-08-09).

4. State Protection

4.1. Legislation

The status and rights of children born out of wedlock are outlined in Guinea's Civil Code (Code civil de la République de Guinée) of 2019 (Guinea 2019), and in the Children's Code (Code de l'enfant) of 2020 (Guinea 2020).

These codes state the following: [translation] "A child conceived and born out of wedlock is natural" (Guinea 2020, Art. 143; Guinea 2019, Art. 404). A child's natural filiation may be established in various ways:

[translation]

Natural filiation is legally established through voluntary recognition.

It may also be legally established by possession of status or through a court judgment.

Proof to the contrary can be shown by any available means. (Guinea 2020, Art. 144; Guinea 2019, Art. 405)

The child's rights are described in the Children's Code as follows: [translation] "A natural child has the same rights and obligations as a legitimate child in their relationship with their father and mother. They enter the family of their parent" (Guinea 2020, Art. 146).

The child's recognition may be made in various ways: [translation] "The recognition of a natural child can be made in the birth certificate, by a certificate received by the civil registrar, by a declaration to the court or by any authentic certificate" (Guinea 2020, Art. 147).

Regarding legitimizing a child born out of wedlock, the Children's Code provides that [translation] "legitimation shall take place either by marriage of the parents or by court decision" (Guinea 2020, Art. 133). The effect of the parents' marriage is described as follows:

[translation]

All children born out of wedlock, including those who are deceased, are legitimized by right by the subsequent marriage of their father and mother.

If their filiation was not already established, these children are recognized at the time of the marriage ceremony. In such cases, the civil registrar conducting the ceremony records the recognition and legitimation in a separate certificate. (Guinea 2020, Art. 134; Guinea 2019, Art. 395)

The Children's Code describes legitimation by court decision as follows:

[translation]

If it appears that marriage between the two parents is impossible, legitimation may still be conferred on the child by judicial authority provided that, with respect to the parent seeking it, the child has the status of natural child. (Guinea 2020, Art. 138)

The Children's Code also provides the following:

[translation]

When the filiation of a natural child has not been established with regard to their father and mother, or either parent, until after their marriage, legitimation can only take place by virtue of a [court] judgment.

This judgment must establish that, since the solemnization of the marriage, the child has had the status of a common child. (Guinea 2020, Art. 135)

Finally, both codes provide the following: [translation] "Any legitimation is noted in the margin of the birth certificate of the legitimized child. This note may be requested by the public prosecutor's office or any interested party" (Guinea 2020, Art. 136; Guinea 2019, Art. 397).

Regarding the rights of the legitimized child, the Children's Code states the following: [translation] "Legitimation confers on a legitimized child the same rights and responsibilities as a legitimate child. It takes effect on the date of the marriage" (Guinea 2020, Art. 137).

Finally, with regard to the inheritance rights of a natural child, the Civil Code provides as follows:

[translation]

Article 689: Natural filiation only creates inheritance rights insofar as it is legally established.

Article 690: A recognized natural child has the same rights as a legitimate child in the succession of their father and mother and other collateral relatives.

...

Article 692: A child born of adultery who is legitimized by the subsequent marriage of their father and mother shall have all the rights of a legitimate descendant.

Article 693: A child born of incest is entitled only to support.

The same applies to a child born of adultery in the absence of legitimation. Where the lack of legitimation is primarily caused by racial or religious discrimination, a natural child shall enjoy all the rights of a legitimate child. (Guinea 2019, bold in original)

4.2. Implementation of Legislation

According to ASMEG's president, the Guinean legal framework aims to officially establish equality between all children, regardless of their parents' situation (2026-08-09). In a 2022 study on child protection in Guinea, Lawyers Without Borders France (Avocats sans frontières France, ASF France) similarly notes that the new Civil Code and the new Children's Code grant natural children the same status and rights as those granted to other children (2022, 12).

According to a human rights defender cited by Radio France internationale (RFI), the [2019] Civil Code now recognizes [translation] "the possibility for children born out of wedlock to receive an inheritance" (2018-12-31). According to a legal consultant interviewed by the online news website Guinee360 in September 2024, natural children have the same inheritance rights as legitimate children [translation] "if they are recognized by their father" (2025-07-15).

ASF France further notes that [translation] "the distinctions between legitimate children, natural children, children born of adultery, and children born of incest are considerably mitigated by articles 691 to 694 of the new Civil Code," which concern succession rights (2022, 25). The legal consultant interviewed for the Guinee360 article nevertheless indicates that [translation] "children born of incest are excluded from succession under the Civil Code. They are only entitled only to support, that is, expenses related to maintenance, food, education, and so on" (2025-07-15). Similarly, a lawyer consulted in January 2026 for a blog post on Judicalex-GN, a website that popularizes Guinean law (Judicalex n.d.), similarly noted the following regarding the succession rights of these individuals:

[translation]

"However, the Guinean Civil Code provides that children born of incest are only entitled to support. This is a provision of the Guinean Civil Code. … Support refers to everything that is necessary for life: food, medicine, clothing and shelter." (Judicalex 2026-01-15)

ASMEG's president stated that, although equality between all children, including those born out of wedlock, is officially recognized by law, [translation] "the reality is quite different" in practice: "[t]he main difference lies in the implementation of rights. Formal law is often superseded by customs and social practices. The equality of rights recognized by law is undermined by social realities" (2026-08-09). The same source added that a child born out of wedlock whose father refuses to recognize them may [translation] "face immense difficulties" when being registered with the civil registry and that, "[w]ithout a birth certificate, the child does not legally exist and is deprived of many fundamental rights (schooling, access to healthcare, etc.)" (ASMEG 2026-08-09).

Lastly, based on its previous research and consulted sources, OFPRA notes that [translation] "[t]he Guinean Children's Code, the Civil Code, the Penal Code [and] the Convention on the Rights of the Child are instruments protecting the rights of mothers and children, but like the body of legislation as a whole, they remain unknown to the population and are relatively seldom enforced" (France 2023-01-20, 3).

4.3. Protection

The OFPRA report notes that Guinea is a signatory to the [UN] Convention on the Rights of the Child, the African Charter on the Rights and Welfare of the Child [of the African Union] and the UN Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination Against Women (France 2023-01-20, 3).

However, Freedom House reports the following:

Due process is upheld unevenly in the state justice system, and many disputes are settled informally through traditional justice systems; these systems also apply due process unevenly, and decisions are not subject to appeal procedures. (Freedom House 2024, Sec. F2)

ASMEG's president similarly noted that [translation] "the police and the justice system are often reluctant to interfere in what they consider to be 'family matters'" and that "the risk of corruption or family pressure is high" (2026-08-09). The same source noted that [translation] "the Guinean state lacks both the means and the political will to enforce the law and protect these individuals" (ASMEG 2026-08-09).

ASMEG's president also stated that [translation] "for a victim, filing a complaint would mean publicly exposing their situation and that of their family, which is socially unacceptable and would subject them to even greater rejection" (2026-08-09).

5. Support Services

ASMEG's president indicated that

[translation]

[s]upport services specifically for persons born out of wedlock are virtually non-existent in Guinea. The state does not offer any such program and community organizations like ASMEG have limited resources available to address these situations. (2026-08-09)

The NGO further stated that

[translation]

[w]e try to provide psychological support and basic legal assistance. But this is still a drop in the bucket compared to the scale of the needs, and we are far from being able to cover the entire national territory. (ASMEG 2026-08-09)

The source also noted that their organization's work [translation] "is concentrated in urban areas where [it has] a presence" (ASMEG 2026-08-09).
