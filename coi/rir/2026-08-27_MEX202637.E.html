Responses to Information Requests

Page Content

​​​​​​​Responses to Information Requests (RIRs) are research reports on country conditions. They are requested by IRB decision-makers.

The database contains a seven-year archive of English and French RIRs. Earlier RIRs may be found on the European Country of Origin Information Network website.

RIR​s published by the IRB on its website may have attachments that are inaccessible due to technical constraints and may include translations of documents originally written in languages other than English or French. To obtain a copy of such attachments and/or translated version of the RIR attachments, please email us.​

Disclaimer

Responses to Information Requests (RIRs) cite publicly accessible information available at the time of publication and within time constraints. A list of references and additional sources consulted are included in each RIR. Sources cited are considered the most current information available as of the date of the RIR.            

RIRs are not, and do not purport to be, conclusive as to the merit of any particular claim for refugee protection. Rather, they are intended to support the refugee determination process. More information on the methodology used by the Research Directorate can be found here.          

The assessment and weight to be given to the information in the RIRs are the responsibility of independent IRB members (decision-makers) after considering the evidence and arguments presented by the parties.           

The information presented in RIRs solely reflects the views and perspectives of the sources cited and does not necessarily reflect the position of the IRB or the Government of Canada.          

27 August 2026

MEX202637.E

Mexico: Ability and motivation of cartels to track and target survivors of human trafficking or forced prostitution, particularly single women, across the country; duration of tracking activities; state protection (2024-August 2026)

Research Directorate, Immigration and Refugee Board of Canada

1. Overview

Mexico's National Registry of Missing and Disappeared Persons (Registro Nacional de Personas Desaparecidas y No Localizadas) indicates that as of 20 August 2026, 28,740 women are missing in Mexico (Mexico 2026-08-20). The German Coordination for Human Rights in Mexico (Deutsche Menschenrechtskoordination Mexiko, DMRKM), a network of NGOs, initiatives, and church agencies (DMRKM n.d.), notes that "many" cases of enforced disappearances "conceal crimes of sexual violence, femicide, forced recruitment, as well as exploitation and human trafficking" (2025-08, 2). A report jointly published by the International Federation for Human Rights (Fédération internationale pour les droits humains, FIDH), the Mexican Forensic Anthropology Team (Equipo Mexicano de Antropología Forense, EMAF) [1] and Idheas Strategic Human Rights Litigation (Idheas Litigio Estratégico en Derechos Humanos, Idheas) [2] notes that the "exploitation of missing women and girls is often for sexual trafficking (forced prostitution)" (FIDH, et al. 2025-03-08, 3).

Drawing on figures provided by World Vision México [3], La Jornada, a Mexican daily newspaper, states that 96% of human trafficking cases go unreported (2026-05-19). Citing the United Nations Office on Drugs and Crime's (UNODC) coordinator on human trafficking, France 24 indicates that between 5% and 10% of human trafficking cases in Mexico are reported (2025-09-29).

According to data from Mexico's Executive Secretariat of the National Public Security System (Secretariado Ejecutivo del Sistema Nacional de Seguridad Pública, SESNSP), 279 women were trafficked from January to June 2026, 832 in 2025, and 559 in 2024 (Mexico 2026-07, 49). Referencing data from the same source, the Network for Children's Rights in Mexico (Red por los Derechos de las Infancias en México, REDIM), made up of nearly 80 civil society organizations in Mexico that promotes children's rights nationwide (REDIM n.d.), states that from January to May 2026, 111 minors, including 75 girls, were trafficked in Mexico (2026-07-09). REDIM further reports that out of the 111 victims, 24 were trafficked for sexual exploitation purposes, 11 for forced labour or services, and 40 for other purposes (2026-07-09). The Citizen Council for the Security and Justice of Mexico City (Consejo Ciudadano para la Seguridad y Justicia de la Ciudad de México, Consejo Ciudadano) [4], drawing from the 6,653 calls made to its human trafficking hotline between January 2025 and July 2026, reports that women and girls represent 67% of the cases, and that 73% of the total calls included child sexual abuse material, and the remaining 27% were of other forms of trafficking, most of which were sexual exploitation (Consejo Ciudadano 2026-07, 2, 12).

1.1 Role of Cartels in Human Trafficking

In an opinion article published by Animal Político, Víctor Manuel Sánchez Valdés, a research professor at the National Autonomous University of Coahuila (Universidad Autónoma de Coahuila) who researches organized crime (Animal Político n.d.), notes that criminal organizations [translation] "control the main prostitution corridors" in Mexico and "are responsible for trafficking women for sexual exploitation" (Sánchez Valdés 2026-05-25). Similarly, according to the US Department of State's Trafficking in Persons Report 2025, organized crime groups traffic children and adults to sexually exploit them (US 2025-09-25). The same source adds that these organizations use "fraudulent job advertisements, economic or social manipulation, torture, threats of murder, blackmail, intimidation, or kidnapping to forc[e] or coerce adults and children into sex trafficking" (US 2025-09-25).

According to a 2023 academic article by Simón Pedro Izcara Palacios, a professor at the Autonomous University of Tamaulipas (Universidad Autónoma de Tamaulipas) who researches human trafficking and smuggling, cartels are a "major player in Mexico's sex trafficking industry" (Izcara Palacios 2023-08-21, 6, 22). In correspondence with the Research Directorate, CrimiPol, a Mexican data analysis consultancy that conducts research on crime and violence (CrimiPol n.d.), reported that [translation] "the control of the human trafficking market" nationwide is "mainly divided" between drug cartels and "family-based dynastic networks" (CrimiPol 2026-08-02). A representative of Consejo Ciudadano's human trafficking program stated in correspondence with the Research Directorate that "the majority of women reported as victims of sexual exploitation appear to have been recruited by smaller networks or by individuals within their immediate or professional environment, including employers, acquaintances, or family members" (Consejo Ciudadano 2026-08-10).

Milenio, a Spanish-language daily newspaper in Mexico, found through court documents and publicly available intelligence, that 9 out of every 10 criminal groups generate income from forced prostitution (2026-05-28). In an interview with Animal Político, the director of the National Network of Shelters (Red Nacional de Refugios, RNR), a network of government and civil society organizations in Mexico (RNR n.d.), indicated that 40% of the human trafficking perpetrators of women supported by the RNR [translation] "have ties to organized crime" (Animal Político 2026-07-28).

According to sources, criminal organizations such as the Jalisco New Generation Cartel (Cartel de Jalisco Nueva Generación, CJNG), the Sinaloa Cartel [and the "remaining factions of the Gulf Cartel (CrimiPol 2026-08-02)] participate directly and systematically in human trafficking (CrimiPol 2026-08-02) or are "increasingly involved" in it (GI-TOC 2025-11-10, Sec. 1). CrimiPol added that these organizations are also involved in forced prostitution and forced labour (2026-08-02). For further information on Mexican cartels, including their activities, see Response to Information Request MEX202636 of September 2026.

2. Legislation

Mexico's 2012 General Law to Prevent, Punish and Eradicate Crimes Related to Human Trafficking (Ley General para Prevenir, Sancionar y Erradicar los Delitos en Materia de Trata de Personas), amended in 2025, provides the following:

[translation]

Article 10. Any wilful act or omission by one or several persons to recruit, lure, transport, transfer, retain, hand over, receive or house one or several persons for the purpose of exploitation shall be punishable by imprisonment for a term of 5 to 15 years and a fine between one thousand and twenty thousand days, without prejudice to the penalties applicable to each of the offences committed, as provided for and punishable under this Act and the relevant penal codes.

Article 13. Any person who profits from the exploitation of one or several persons through prostitution, pornography, public or private sexual performances, sex tourism or any other paid sexual activity by the following means shall be punished by imprisonment for a term of 15 to 30 years and a fine of one thousand to thirty thousand days:

  1. Deception;
  2. Physical or psychological violence;
  3. Abuse of power;
  4. Taking advantage of a situation of vulnerability;
  5. Serious harm or the threat of serious harm; or
  6. The threat of reporting the victim to the authorities regarding their immigration status in the country or any other abuse of the law or legal procedures that causes the victim to submit to the perpetrator's demands.

In the case of minors or persons who lack the capacity to understand the significance of the act, proof of the means mentioned in this article shall not be required. (Mexico 2012, emphasis in original)

Mexico's 2013 General Law on Victims (Ley General de Víctimas), amended in 2024, provides the following regarding the recognition of victims' status:

[translation]

Article 111. Recognition of victim status will have the following effects:

Access to the rights, guarantees, remedies, mechanisms and procedures provided for in this Law and the regulatory provisions, and

In cases of serious injury; offences against sexual freedom; domestic violence; trafficking in persons; kidnapping; torture; cruel, inhuman or degrading treatment; disappearance; deprivation of liberty; and any other offences that, by reason of the nature of the harm suffered, prevent victims from adequately safeguarding their rights, the judge hearing the case or the authority responsible for the proceeding shall immediately suspend all judicial and administrative proceedings, suspend the running of any limitation or peremptory periods and suspend all resulting effects for as long as those circumstances persist, provided that it is demonstrated that the victim is unable to adequately exercise their rights in those judicial and administrative proceedings.

Upon recognition of victim status, victims may access the Support Resources and full reparation in accordance with this Law and its Regulations. The applicable Regulations will determine the procedure and the elements to be demonstrated. (Mexico 2013, emphasis in original)

3. Ability of Cartels to Track and Target Survivors of Human Trafficking

According to Izcara Palacios, "the association between prostitution networks, cartels and police authorities is an amalgam that inhibits reporting and makes it difficult for victims to escape" (2023-08-21, 10). According to the Global Initiative Against Transnational Organized Crime (GI-TOC), an "independent civil-society organization, headquartered in Geneva" (GI-TOC n.d.), corruption at "various levels of government" enables cartels to operate "with relative impunity, often receiving protection or even direct assistance" (2025-11-10). The Latin American branch of A21, a US-headquartered global NGO that combats human trafficking (A21 n.d.), stated the following in correspondence with the Research Directorate:

When exploitation is linked to organized criminal groups or drug cartels, the risks faced by survivors increase substantially. These groups often have extensive territorial presence, the capacity to carry out reprisals, the resources to locate victims, and, in some contexts, corruption networks that may undermine confidence in public institutions and make both reporting and reintegration more difficult. (A21 2026-07-22)

The information in the following paragraph was provided by CrimiPol:

Cartels [translation] "routinely confiscate" their victims' original identity documents to control their movements. They also provide victims with smartphones that have "built-in passive GPS tracking software" to track their movements in real time. Furthermore, cartels monitor social media activity to extract "Exif data (hidden metadata containing geolocation) from digital photographs." They also rely on large networks of "informants and corrupt local authorities, including police and judicial officials," to obtain information on the survivor's location should she, for instance, seek assistance at a local police station, visit her hometown, or contact a family member (CrimiPol 2026-08-02).

For further information on the ability and means of cartels to track individuals, see Response to Information Request MEX202636 of September 2026.

3.1. Motivation to Track and Target Survivors, Including Duration of Tracking Activities

Information on the motivation of cartels to track and target single women who are survivors of human trafficking or forced prostitution, including the duration of tracking activities, was scarce among the sources consulted by the Research Directorate within the time constraints of this Response.

The information in the following paragraphs was provided by CrimiPol:

Cartels are motivated to track survivors because of [translation] "a combination of financial greed and operational security." These organizations perceive a human trafficking victim as a "renewable commodity that generates continuous and exponential profits." Cartels view survivors as lost income and as a "serious" legal and security threat because their testimony could divulge the organization's logistics, safe houses, financial operations, and connections to corrupt officials. Tracking survivors enables cartels to "enforce silence through reprisals, often by threatening, extorting, or harming the survivor's family members who remain in Mexico."

A cartel's motivation to track a survivor [translation] "can persist indefinitely." Because many trafficking networks function as "highly territorial and militarized cartels, or as intergenerational criminal families," they are motivated to track a survivor as long as they perceive them to be a legal threat, either due to cooperation with the authorities or due to an outstanding debt (CrimiPol 2026-08-02). Corroborating information could not be found among the sources consulted by the Research Directorate within the time constraints of this Response.

4. State Protection

According to sources, the following state protection mechanisms are available to survivors of human trafficking and forced prostitution:

  • The Executive Commission for Victim Assistance (Comisión Ejecutiva de Atención a Víctimas, Ceav), a [federal government (Idheas 2024-01, 2)] organization that provides "access to legal assistance, medical and psychological care, and, in certain cases, financial support" (A21 2026-07-22);
  • Women's Justice Centres (Centros de Justicia para las Mujeres) (ÁGAPE 2026-07-29; A21 2026-07-22; Mexico 2024-10-31), established by the government to improve access to justice for survivors of violence (Mexico 2024-10-31). As of 2024, there were 73 such centres (Mexico 2024-10-31);
  • Government-operated shelters (Consejo Ciudadano 2026-08-10; ÁGAPE 2026-07-29; US 2025-09-25);
  • Specialized prosecutors' offices (Consejo Ciudadano 2026-08-10; ÁGAPE 2026-07-29).

Sources indicate that Mexico City has a specialized shelter for women and children who have been trafficked (Refugio Especializado para Mujeres, Niñas y Niños Víctimas del Delito de Trata de Personas), inaugurated in 2016, which provides medical, psychological, legal, and social support to human trafficking survivors (Milenio 2026-05-25; Infobae 2026-06-02), and has the capacity shelter up to 40 survivors (Milenio 2026-05-25). According to Infobae, a Spanish-language news website from Argentina (The Washington Post 2016-06-08), survivors must be referred to the shelter by the public prosecutor's office through the issuance of a [translation] "protective measure" (Infobae 2026-06-02).

Citing an unnamed "international organization," the US Trafficking in Persons Report 2025 notes that there are 15 government-funded "specialized shelters" for human trafficking survivors, including 8 that are managed by NGOs (US 2025-09-25). The same source states that "[m]edical and psychological support" provided by shelters "often did not extend beyond cursory evaluations," adding that "[s]helters at the state and local levels typically housed victims only for the duration of a criminal trial and long-term reintegration services were very limited" (US 2025-09-25). In correspondence with the Research Directorate, the founder and Executive Director of ÁGAPE, a Mexico City-based NGO that raises awareness on gender-based violence and human trafficking (ÁGAPE n.d.), stated that human trafficking survivors "continue to face delays in identification, limited availability of specialized shelters, insufficient long-term reintegration services, and barriers to obtaining timely and effective protection measures" (ÁGAPE 2026-07-29). The source added that many survivors only gain access to specialized shelters after repeated failed attempts to obtain assistance from public authorities or after not "initially [being] identified as victims of trafficking" (ÁGAPE 2026-07-29).

According to GI-TOC, despite "some efforts" made to counter human trafficking, enforcement continues to be a challenge due to "systemic corruption and inefficiencies within the judiciary" (2025-11-10). A21 indicated that the existence of state protection mechanisms "does not always translate into effective protection," as "implementation is often limited by significant disparities among states, insufficient resources, lengthy response times, limited specialized capacity for the identification of and assistance to victims, and uneven interinstitutional coordination" (2026-07-02). ÁGAPE similarly noted that access to state protection mechanisms "varies considerably" across Mexico based on "the institutional capacity of each state, the availability of specialized services, and effective inter-agency coordination" (2026-07-30). According to the same source, some of the "significant structural challenges" that remain for survivors of human trafficking include

inconsistent implementation across jurisdictions, limited specialized resources, insufficient long-term protection and reintegration programs, low prosecution and conviction rates relative to the scale of the crime, and persistent concerns regarding corruption, impunity, and victims' lack of trust in public authorities. (ÁGAPE 2026-07-29)

This Response was prepared after researching publicly accessible information currently available to the Research Directorate within time constraints.
