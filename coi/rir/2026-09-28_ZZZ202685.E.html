​​​​​​​Responses to Information Requests (RIRs) are research reports on country conditions. They are requested by IRB decision-makers.

The database contains a seven-year archive of English and French RIRs. Earlier RIRs may be found on the European Country of Origin Information Network website.

RIR​s published by the IRB on its website may have attachments that are inaccessible due to technical constraints and may include translations of documents originally written in languages other than English or French. To obtain a copy of such attachments and/or translated version of the RIR attachments, please email us.​

Disclaimer

Responses to Information Requests (RIRs) cite publicly accessible information available at the time of publication and within time constraints. A list of references and additional sources consulted are included in each RIR. Sources cited are considered the most current information available as of the date of the RIR.            

RIRs are not, and do not purport to be, conclusive as to the merit of any particular claim for refugee protection. Rather, they are intended to support the refugee determination process. More information on the methodology used by the Research Directorate can be found here.          

The assessment and weight to be given to the information in the RIRs are the responsibility of independent IRB members (decision-makers) after considering the evidence and arguments presented by the parties.           

The information presented in RIRs solely reflects the views and perspectives of the sources cited and does not necessarily reflect the position of the IRB or the Government of Canada.          

28 September 2026

ZZZ202685.E

Canada & China: Monitoring of Chinese citizens in Canada by Chinese authorities; treatment of family members in China by authorities based on relatives' alleged activities in Canada (2024–September 2026)

Research Directorate, Immigration and Refugee Board of Canada

1. Monitoring of Chinese Citizens in Canada by Chinese Authorities

The final report of the Foreign Interference Commission, which was appointed by the Government of Canada in 2023 (Foreign Interference Commission n.d.), provides the following information concerning Chinese surveillance of citizens abroad:

The [People's Republic of China (PRC)] targets members of Chinese Canadian diaspora communities for the purposes of repression, influence and forced return of targeted individuals to the PRC. It deploys a wide range of tradecraft to carry out its activities, one of which is to use a person's family and friends living in the PRC as leverage against them. The PRC uses its diplomatic missions, PRC international students, community organizations and private individuals, among others, to carry out its transnational repression [(TNR)] [1] activities. (Foreign Interference Commission 2025-01-28, 98)

In correspondence with the Research Directorate, a team of researchers at the Citizen Lab [2] based at the University of Toronto stated the following concerning Chinese surveillance of individuals in Canada:

Chinese authorities and their proxies routinely monitor the political activities of select … Chinese citizens living in Canada, as well as members of diaspora communities who are Canadian citizens. The goal of this on- and offline surveillance, along with more coercive forms of transnational repression, is to silence overseas opposition to the Chinese government and any critical voices that challenge the Chinese government's preferred political narratives. (Researchers 2026-09-11, 7)

A May 2025 article by CBC News, based on a joint investigation it conducted alongside the International Consortium of Investigative Journalists (ICIJ) [3], notes that Chinese dissidents in Canada are "targeted" by Chinese officials for articulating views contrary to those of the Chinese government on what it calls the "'five poisons': democracy in Hong Kong, treatment of Uyghurs, Tibetan freedom, the Falun Gong and Taiwanese independence" (2025-05-29). In correspondence with the Research Directorate, a full professor of political science at the University of Alberta, who focuses on civil societies in China, noted that while Chinese authorities are known to monitor Chinese citizens in Canada, the prevalence of this monitoring is "not fully known"(Full Professor 2026-09-09).

According to the researchers, Chinese authorities and their proxies draw on a "range of on- and offline surveillance techniques" (Researchers 2026-09-11, 5). The CBC-ICIJ joint investigation states that "several" Chinese dissidents report being "followed and surveilled in Canada," and notes that "attacks by the Chinese government on dissidents living in Canada" and other countries are "on the rise" (CBC 2025-05-29). The same article adds that the dissidents CBC spoke with also reported receiving "[t]hreatening phone calls" and experiencing online "attacks" such as "spamouflage," in which a "bot network" is used to "push spam-like content and propaganda across multiple social media platforms" (CBC 2025-05-29). In an interview with the Research Directorate, a professor emeritus at a Canadian university, who specializes in security in East Asia, stated that there are varied techniques that Chinese authorities will deploy in monitoring an individual in Canada, but it depends on the citizenship status of the individual or how long they have been away from China (2026-09-02).

1.1. Profiles of Individuals Monitored

In an interview with the Research Directorate, the China in the World Director at Safeguard Defenders [4] described the following ranking of Chinese people vulnerable to Chinese state monitoring in Canada:

  • "highest risk category": former officials and employees of Chinese state-owned companies, owing to concerns of "disloyalty" and their possession of "proprietary information"
  • second highest risk category: dissidents, members of the "younger generation," social media influencers, and individuals who possess "a voice in China," as well as anyone who "interacts with" such personalities
  • third highest risk group: "the older generation of human rights defenders" (Safeguard Defenders 2026-09-02).

The Professor Emeritus indicated that some targets of Chinese monitoring in Canada are economic, including those engaged in "commercial transactions gone wrong" or "those facing criminal charges" in China, whereas others are political, such as "those attacking the regime's legitimacy," those "critici[zing]" the Chinese Communist Party (CCP) and its "system of rule" (including Falun Gong practitioners), and people who disagree with the international politics of the PRC (2026-09-02). The same source added that those who publish written material critical of China will draw surveillance, particularly if they are considered "repeat offenders" (Professor Emeritus 2026-09-02). According to the Full Professor, the profiles of Chinese people that PRC authorities monitor in Canada generally include Hong Kongers, Taiwanese, Uyghurs, Tibetans and former PRC officials, with the last category drawing "acute interest" if the former official is alleged to have committed economic crimes (2026-09-09). The researchers noted that individuals in Canada whom Chinese authorities monitor "tend to resemble" those they surveil inside China, which fall into the following groups:

  • pro-democracy and human rights campaigners, exiled political dissidents, and civil society activists;
  • students, scholars, writers, journalists, artists, social media influencers, and new media professionals who express anti-Communist Party views or opinions at odds with the Chinese government's preferred narratives;
  • members of ethnic or religious communities, including (but not limited to) Tibetans, Uyghurs, Mongolians, and Falun Gong practitioners;
  • supporters of, or participants in, Hong Kong's pro-democracy protest movement;
  • individuals, including current or former Communist Party members and state employees, who are accused of corruption, financial crimes, and/or official malfeasance; and
  • the family members of any of the above individuals (2026-09-11, 8−9).

The same source added that individuals belonging to the above-mentioned groups may be targeted even if they don't publicly broadcast their view on the Chinese government (Researchers 2026-09-11, 9).

The researchers further indicated that those targeted "are not always highly public figures," and provided the following examples of actions that may trigger surveillance:

Some [of those targeted] may use various forms of public advocacy to openly criticize the Chinese government, and may do so as an individual or as a member of civil society organizations. Others may express their political views on social media under their real name or pseudonymously, or may privately communicate their political opinions with fellow Chinese citizens or diaspora members via text message, email, or direct messages on (un)encrypted messaging apps. Others may have faced state persecution in China, and now in Canada refrain from any on- or offline activities abroad which they believe would expose them or their China-based family members to reprisals from Chinese authorities. (2026-09-11, 9)

The same source noted that an individual surveilled in Canada who is "compelled" to return to China could face "detention and/or prosecution" in China based on the information collected on their "political activities" in Canada (Researchers 2026-09-11, 9).

1.2. Chinese State Actors Involved in Monitoring

The Foreign Interference Commission final report notes that China draws on a "wide range of actors" for foreign interference in Canada (2025-01-28, 40). Sources indicate that China's Ministry of State Security (MSS) and Ministry of Public Security (MPS), [including their offices at the local and provincial levels (Researchers 2026-09-11, 24)], are involved in overseas surveillance (Foreign Interference Commission 2025-01-28, 40; researchers 2026-09-11, 24). The Foreign Interference Commission notes that China also commits its actions of foreign interference through its diplomatic officials (2025-01-28, 40). The Full Professor indicated that the agencies "most consistently involved" in surveillance in Canada are the MSS and its state security bureaus at the provincial or municipal levels responsible for "political intelligence abroad and dissident cases"; the "operating unit" is "normally" the bureau associated with the targeted individual's "home locality" in China (2026-09-09). The same source added that the MPS and local Public Security Bureaus (PSBs), as well as the United Front Work Department, are also involved to "some" extent, "notably by cultivating contact with community associations, diaspora media and student organizations" (Full Professor 2026-09-09).

According to a June 2025 CBC article, the United Front Work Department is "considered by numerous national security experts to be the Chinese Communist Party's main interference tool both at home and abroad" (2025-06-14). The Foreign Interference Commission final report notes that the United Front Work Department "tries to control and influence Chinese diaspora communities, shape international opinions and influence politicians to support PRC policies" (2025-01-28, 40). A report on the United Front Work Department by Cheryl Yu, a fellow in Chinese studies at the Jamestown Foundation, a US-based think tank focusing on global developments of strategic relevance to the US (Jamestown Foundation n.d.), states that through the department, the CCP has "created a global network of individuals and organizations as part of its united front system," including through the "protracted co-optation of existing civil society organizations overseas" and the integration of them into "the overseas united front system" (Yu 2026-02-11, 2, 4, 6). These organizations, Yu adds, which range from identity-based organizations to cultural promotion centres and friendship associations to student organizations, then "become voices and proxies for the Party during key moments," and members of these organizations have been active in working to "harass and monitor Chinese dissidents and activists" and "interrupt events that are alleged to be 'discrediting China'" (2026-02-11, 18-32). According to Yu, there are 575 United Front-linked organizations in Canada (2026-02-11, 18, 20). The same source indicates that the CCP draws on its overseas united front network to "carry out transnational repression, by monitoring, harassing, and directly attacking those it considers to be threats" (Yu 2026-02-11, 45). The researchers stated that "[m]ost" overseas organizations in Canada and other countries that are affiliated with the United Front are not involved in TNR and are instead "focused on non-coercive influence operations within Chinese diaspora communities which aim to build consent for the Chinese government and its preferred political narratives" (2026-09-11, 25). The same source, however, added that there have been some "notable cases" in which individuals associated with United Front-linked organizations in North America "have been implicated in efforts to monitor and harass overseas Chinese citizens and diaspora members" (Researchers 2026-09-11, 25).

Safeguard Defenders noted that the management teams of Chinese companies operating abroad are also "often closely tied" with the United Front Work Department, and they are aware of reports of employee passports being withheld by such companies (2026-09-02). Corroborating information could not be found among the sources consulted by the Research Directorate within the time constraints of this Response.

1.3. Monitoring Methods and Approaches

A 2025 ICIJ investigation on China's TNR reviewed a 2004 Chinese police textbook and Chinese government guidelines for domestic security officers from 2013, finding that the "tactics recently deployed against" opponents abroad "mirrored the guidelines on how to control individuals labeled as domestic security threats" (2025-04-28). Safeguard Defenders noted that China has developed abroad a "multi-layered system" of "widespread community surveillance," which "involves officials at all levels of government" and civilians who "share information with authorities" (2026-09-02). The Professor Emeritus noted that individuals who are "proxies independent of the state and Party agencies, but acting at their behest," can be involved in monitoring (2026-09-02). The Foreign Interference Commission final report states that Chinese authorities rely on "proxies, individuals or organizations," each of whom take "explicit or implicit direction" from Beijing to pursue foreign interference (2025-01-28, 40). According to the researchers, Chinese authorities have "recruited people in North America and Europe, including both diaspora and non-diaspora members, to monitor and even threaten targeted Chinese citizens and diaspora members"; these "threats" are used as "intimidation to compel" targeted individuals to "return to China and surrender to local authorities" there (2026-09-11, 24-25). The same source added that Chinese authorities have also "recruited members of overseas dissident organizations to act as informants," and have "attempted to enlist members of persecuted ethnic and religious minorities" to serve the same function (Researchers 2026-09-11, 25).

The May 2025 CBC article quotes a former Canadian Security Intelligence Service (CSIS) intelligence officer as stating that because Chinese authorities consider "'a lot of the main threats to their dominance'" to originate from abroad, they "'need to go abroad to basically neutralize them'" (2025-05-29). The former CSIS officer added that whereas previously China may have attempted to "bring a dissident back to China," now Chinese authorities draw on "more sophisticated" "surveillance and tactics" (CBC 2025-05-29).

A report by based on a Canada-wide public opinion survey jointly conducted by the China Governance Lab [5] and Digital Public Square [6], and authored by Lynette Ong [7], which sampled 2,000 Canada-based adults in June 2024—including an oversampling of those who identified as "having any Chinese, Hong Kong, Taiwanese, Tibetan, or Uyghur heritage" [8]—found that 38% of 443 respondents belonging to the Chinese, Hong Konger, Uyghur, Tibetan or Taiwanese racial or cultural groups reported having heard of "any intimidation, harassment, or covert threats from a foreign government or its agents happening in [their] cultural, religious, or ethnic community" in Canada (China Governance Lab & Digital Public Square 2024-11-14, 5, 7, 19). Of those 443 respondents, 5% had personally experienced "any intimidation, harassment, or covert threats from a foreign government or its agents," and 9% knew a family member or personal friend who had (China Governance Lab & Digital Public Square 2024-11-14, 19). Amongst those who reported having "heard of or experienced intimidation in their community," 53% received "online harassment and threats," 40% reported "threatening phone calls," 33% experienced "harassment to family members," 31% listed "physical threats," and 18% noted "visits from the police" (China Governance Lab & Digital Public Square 2024-11-14, 20).

The Full Professor noted that digital monitoring is the "predominant" method of Chinese state surveillance of individuals in Canada, including reported cases of "phishing [online scam] and digital impersonation targeting Uyghur, Tibetan, Taiwanese and Hong Kong diaspora activists and journalists" who cover those groups (2026-09-09). The researchers described the following 4 "major techniques of overseas online surveillance":

  • "social media monitoring," which occurs on "Western" platforms as well as "China-based" ones, such as WeChat and RedNote. Authorities scan user content across these platforms, note accounts "of interest," and monitor the activities of those accounts as well as the accounts that engage with them;
  • "impersonation and account takeover," which involves authorities or their proxies gaining access to private group chats by creating a fake online identity, impersonating an existing group member, or gaining control of an existing group member's account;
  • "malware and spyware," which are used to gain complete control over a target's electronic device, thereby accessing sensitive personal and professional data;
  • "social engineering and phishing," which involves tricking targets into revealing sensitive information, breaking security protocols, or installing malicious software onto their devices (2026-09-11, 16-20).

The Professor Emeritus indicated that the "technological instruments for surveillance and monitoring are increasingly sophisticated and available to state and non-state actors alike" (2026-09-02). The same source further noted that there is "close monitoring" by Chinese authorities of social media applications, with WeChat being the "most important" (Professor Emeritus 2026-09-02). Safeguard Defenders indicated that Chinese authorities "continuously" monitor the WeChat platform, but they also surveil Western social media platforms, noting that an individual "following, or interacting with, certain" social media users can draw attention from Chinese authorities; Safeguard Defenders is aware of cases in which a person based in China or a person returning to China has been "interrogated" for having "interacted with certain social media accounts" (Safeguard Defenders 2026-09-02). Corroborating information could not be found among the sources consulted by the Research Directorate within the time constraints of this Response.

The Full Professor stated that "[p]hysical observation" and "disruption" of events deemed unfavourable to China are other approaches Chinese authorities use in monitoring individuals in Canada (2026-09-09). According to the researchers, Chinese citizens and diaspora members participating in public events in Canada concerning Chinese politics, human rights, or related issues "are vulnerable to surveillance" (2026-09-11, 22). The same source adds that the monitoring is conducted by Chinese authorities and those who support the Chinese government; such non-state actors "may not work under direct orders from Chinese authorities," but have instead "self-selected to report on the activities of overseas Chinese citizens or diaspora members" (Researchers 2026-09-11, 22). The Full Professor stated that, in Canada, Chinese state surveillance is "concentrat[ed]" in Greater Toronto, Metro Vancouver, and Montreal, noting that these three regions are home to the "majority" of the Chinese diaspora and have a Chinese consular presence (2026-09-09).

1.4. Incidents of Monitoring

In a May 2026 article, Rapid Response Mechanism (RRM) Canada—a component of Global Affairs Canada's efforts to counter foreign interference by tracking information threats against the country (Canada 2026-08-31)—states that it has detected an ongoing "'Spamouflage'" campaign, in effect since 31 August 2024, that it has "high confidence" is being carried out by Chinese authorities (Canada 2026-05-11). The same source adds that the campaign involves doctored (or deepfake) videos of a Chinese-speaking online commentator in Canada, who has previously posted content critical of China, which are posted 100-200 times per day in the comments sections of social media sites (Canada 2026-05-11). The campaign also includes the targeting of 9 other Chinese-speaking online personalities in Canada, whose private information—including their phone number, email address and home address—was made public without their consent (Canada 2026-05-11).

The information in the following 3 paragraphs was provided in the May 2025 CBC article:

An Ottawa-based advocate for China's Uyghur minority who, upon visiting Montreal, was notified by an employee of Global Affairs Canada that 2 cars with concealed licence plates were following him. The same advocate later had 3 cellphones and a laptop computer "attacked"; after reporting it to the RCMP and the Citizen Lab, it was concluded that the attack originated in China.

A Toronto-based man who leads a "pro-democracy group" in the city stated that his group's members are "often reluctant to grant interviews or openly participate in his organization," noting that "'almost all'" of these members fear that their families in China "would have their jobs or pensions threatened" were they to express "public opposition to the Chinese government." The same group leader adds that when his group holds pro-democracy events at Toronto's City Hall or in front of the Chinese consulate, "they are watched, with people taking photos and videos."

A Canada-based Hong Kong democracy activist who "received phone calls warning her to stop interfering with Hong Kong's and China's affairs" and was sent "notices from Google about attempts by state-level hackers to get into her computer systems" (CBC 2025-05-29).

In the summer of 2024, the Foreign Interference Commission met [9] with individuals representing several different Canadian communities, including the Chinese Canadian diaspora, Falun Gong practitioners, the Uyghur Canadian diaspora, and the Tibetan Canadian diaspora (Foreign Interference Commission [2024]a). In the Commission's meeting with 5 individuals from the Chinese Canadian diaspora, respondents reported being "monitored, harassed, and threatened" because of their advocacy work for Hong Kong, and spoke of experiences including receiving "detailed, graphic, and harrowing death threats, monitoring and surveillance, and harassment online and in person" (Foreign Interference Commission [2024]b, 1, 2). In the Commission's meeting with 6 members of the Hong Kong Canadian community, "[c]ertain" participants noted that the CCP "and its proxies" used tactics including "spreading misinformation and disinformation, organizing counter-protests, following people who attend protests and rallies, surveillance and photographing people, death threats and threats of sexual violence made in anonymous phone calls, and physical violence" to "threaten, harass, and intimidate" members of the Chinese Canadian community (Foreign Interference Commission [2024]c, 1, 2). In a separate meeting with 4 individuals from the Hong Kong Canadian community, "[c]ertain" attendees told the Commission that Canadian Hong Kongers are "monitored" in Canada and provided the Commission with examples from both personal and community experience (Foreign Interference Commission [2024]d, 1, 2).

In the first of two meetings with members of Canada's Falun Gong community, the Commission heard from 8 members, some of whom reported being "harassed and monitored by the CCP and its agents in Canada," and some who spoke of treatment they faced whilst protesting against the PRC and the CCP including "acts of verbal and physical violence directed at the practitioners, their protest materials being destroyed, vandalized or stolen, and protesters being photographed and filmed by unknown individuals" (Foreign Interference Commission [2024]e, 1, 2). In the Commission's second meeting with members of the Falun Gong community, certain of the 8 attendees reported "being harassed, including in person and over the phone, by people unknown to them who knew private details" about their personal lives (Foreign Interference Commission [2024]f, 1, 2).

According to the Foreign Interference Commission's summary of a meeting with members of the Uyghur Canadian community, some of the 3 attendees indicated that their activities were being "monitored by the PRC in Canada," that they "assume their electronic devices are compromised," and that they "regularly receive threatening phone calls from anonymous Chinese speakers" (Foreign Interference Commission [2024]g, 1, 2).

In a summary of its meeting with 2 members of the Tibetan Canadian community, the Foreign Interference Commission reports that attendees noted Tibetan Community members who are "actively working to raise awareness about Tibet," particularly who "still have loved ones inside Tibet," are "targets for transnational repression" by the PRC (Foreign Interference Commission [2024]h, 1, 2).

2. Treatment of Family Members in China by Authorities Based on Relatives' Alleged

Activities in Canada

2.1. Methods and Approaches

Canada's Privy Council Office provides the following information concerning the Chinese government's use of PRC-based family members as a tool of TNR in Canada:

It exploits PRC-based family members to pressure those in Canada to cease certain activities the PRC views as hostile, or to return to the PRC. It also threatens PRC-based family members with a range of potential coercive actions, including detention or financial penalties. (Canada 2025-04-21, notes omitted)

According to the researchers, Chinese authorities are "known to intimidate the China-based family members of Chinese citizens or diaspora members in Canada" (2026-09-11, 27). The Full Professor indicated that "leveraging family members inside China is the most extensively documented mechanism" Chinese authorities use, and authorities even "describe it" as a "preferred strategy" "in their own official materials" (2026-09-09). The same source added that such "coercive measures against family members" are "paired with direct intimidation of the target overseas" to "persuade the individual under scrutiny abroad to return" to China (Full Professor 2026-09-09).

According to the ICIJ investigation, which draws on interviews by media organizations across 23 countries with 105 targets of Chinese transnational repression, one tactic Chinese authorities use to "stifle dissent overseas" is called "'emotional influence of kinship'," under which an individual's family members are pressured to encourage that person to cease their activism (2025-04-28). The researchers noted that threats against the China-based family of targets in Canada are used to "demonstrate the Chinese government's control over" those relatives in an effort to "warn activists that continuing public advocacy against the Communist Party would further imperil the well-being of their family members" (2026-09-11, 28). Safeguard Defenders indicated that it is familiar with cases in which family members in China have been "pressured" to tell their relatives in Canada to cease following certain social media accounts (2026-09-02). The same source added that in some cases an individual's personal information in China will be publicized, in an effort by authorities to "call on people" to visit the family homes of the individual or their family members (Safeguard Defenders 2026-09-02). The source further stated that they are also familiar with some cases in which a person's family members have been sent abroad to try and convince the individual to return to China (Safeguard Defenders 2026-09-02). Corroborating information could not be found among the sources consulted by the Research Directorate within the time constraints of this Response.

Citing Michael Kovrig—a former Canadian diplomat and an expert on Asia who was detained in China from 2018-2021—the May 2025 CBC article notes that China "tends to target Chinese diaspora communities more because it is easier to intimidate people who have relatives back in China or who belong to a community where many people are sympathetic to the CCP" (2025-05-29). The Full Professor indicated that "[f]amily-directed pressure" tends to be the tool Chinese officials prefer when the pursued individual abroad is "beyond physical reach and not prominent enough to warrant costlier measures"; in this regard, this instrument "reaches considerably further down the profile scale than any other tactic, which inverts the intuition that a low profile means low risk" (2026-09-09). The former CSIS intelligence officer, quoted in the May 2025 CBC article, notes that authorities "'may approach extended family members in the PRC, starting with a subtle message'," before eventually suggesting to them that their relative in Canada is "'doing anti-state activity'" and their family in China could "'lose a job'" as a result if they do not curb their activities (2025-05-29).

According to the researchers, targets in Canada often learn of threats to their China-based family members by Chinese authorities via "phone or video call, direct message, email, or other digital communication platforms" (2026-09-11, 27-28). The Full Professor provided the following "sequence" of actions Chinese authorities may take towards a pursued individual's family members in China, noting that while the sequence is "broadly consistent," not all cases proceed through each step:

1. Initial contact with parents, a spouse or siblings by the local police station or its domestic security protection unit.

2. A home visit or summons for questioning covering the individual's activities, associates, immigration status and finances abroad.

3. Instructions to the family to telephone or video call the person overseas, often with officers present and the call recorded, directing them to delete material, cease attending events, sever ties with named organizations, return to China or provide information on others.

4. Administrative penalties for the family, including loss of state sector employment, suspension of pensions or benefits, denial of school placement for children, obstruction of licences and permits, and restrictions on property or bank accounts;

5. Confiscation or non-renewal of relatives' passports and the imposition of exit bans under the Exit and Entry Administration Law.

6. In escalated cases, residential surveillance at a designated location … or criminal detention on "elastic charges" such as picking quarrels and provoking trouble, subversion of state power, or separatism. (2026-09-09)

The same source added that the above measures are executed locally, in the location of the family's household registration and involving resident or village committees or workplace party organs, and there can be "a lot of variability in terms of treatment across China" as implementation rests with the home locality rather than from the central government in Beijing (Full Professor 2026-09-09). The researchers stated that "legal threats" are also used against the China-based family members, with authorities threatening to "leverage police investigations and criminal convictions to deter exiles and diaspora members from continuing to speak out against the Chinese government" (2026-09-11, 28).

The Full Professor stated that in the "large majority of cases," local PSBs and their domestic security protection units are the lead state actors in cases of family pressure, responsible for "the home visits, summonses and supervised calls," while "residents' committees, village committees and workplace party organs deliver the employment, housing, schooling and benefits consequences"; it is the latter actors who "make the pressure continuous" (2026-09-09). According to the researchers, "numerous" Chinese state offices and proxies participate in the "coercion-by-proxy" strategy of pressuring the China-based family members of targets in Canada, including the MSS and MPS, their corresponding provincial or local level offices and the Hong Kong Police Force; overseas proxies—including Chinese citizens and diaspora members, but also "individuals outside of targeted diaspora communities"—have also been leveraged "to communicate threats to overseas targets" concerning their China-based family members (2026-09-11, 27).

2.2. Incidents of Family Targeting

Canada's Foreign Interference Commission noted that, in its summer 2024 meetings with members the Chinese Canadian community and with Falun Gong practitioners, participants reported having had threats made against their "loved ones" in Canada and in China (Foreign Interference Commission [2024]b, 2; Foreign Interference Commission [2024]e, 2). In the Commission's meeting with members of the Hong Kong Canadian community, one attendee provided examples of Chinese Canadians "who saw consequences for their actions in Canada visited upon their loved ones living in the … PRC" (Foreign Interference Commission [2024]d, 2). Members of the Uyghur Canadian community also informed the Commission of threats being issued against their family members in "East Turkistan"—the area also known as the Xinjiang Uyghur Autonomous Region within the PRC—and certain attendees described their "family members and loved ones being imprisoned, interrogated, disappeared, killed, and sent to concentration camps" (Foreign Interference Commission [2024]g, 2). According to the Full Professor, for cases involving families of Uyghur and Tibetan individuals abroad, the "ceiling of coercion" is "materially higher" (2026-09-09).

Safeguard Defenders noted that Chinese authorities' treatment of family members of those monitored in Canada can include bringing those family members in for questioning, charging them with offenses in place of the individual themselves, family members losing their jobs, or other "bullying techniques" such as losing their access to housing, education (for children), employment, pension or healthcare rights, or restrictions being placed on their freedom of movement (both in China and abroad) (2026-09-02). The Professor Emeritus indicated that China-based family members are invited "for tea" [a euphemism for a summons by the authorities (RSF 2024-02-07)] at a local police bureau station, using this as a message to their relative abroad that they are being monitored there (2026-09-02). The Full Professor noted that targeted individuals in Canada whose family members work for the state sector, or are dependant on state pensions, housing, or benefits are "most vulnerable" to pressure being placed on their family members in China (2026-09-09).

The information in the following 2 paragraphs was provided in the May 2025 CBC article:

Several "Canadian activists who have spoken out" against China reported their family members in China having been questioned by police. Some "victims in Canada" declined CBC's requests for interviews because they "feared repercussions on themselves or their families."

After travelling to Taiwan to "support its independence," a Quebec-based woman had her aunt visited by Chinese national police in the town of Chifeng. The woman also had "[s]exually explicit, deepfake images" of herself circulated online, "an attack" that Canadian officials attribute to the Chinese government. Private information concerning her and her family—information "only the Chinese government" would possess—was posted on a "website listing people who weren't loyal to China," and false rumours "designed to discredit" her and her family were circulated online (CBC 2025-05-29).
