Responses to Information Requests

​​​​​​​Responses to Information Requests (RIRs) are research reports on country conditions. They are requested by IRB decision-makers.

The database contains a seven-year archive of English and French RIRs. Earlier RIRs may be found on the European Country of Origin Information Network website.

RIR​s published by the IRB on its website may have attachments that are inaccessible due to technical constraints and may include translations of documents originally written in languages other than English or French. To obtain a copy of such attachments and/or translated version of the RIR attachments, please email us.​

Disclaimer

Responses to Information Requests (RIRs) cite publicly accessible information available at the time of publication and within time constraints. A list of references and additional sources consulted are included in each RIR. Sources cited are considered the most current information available as of the date of the RIR.            

RIRs are not, and do not purport to be, conclusive as to the merit of any particular claim for refugee protection. Rather, they are intended to support the refugee determination process. More information on the methodology used by the Research Directorate can be found here.          

The assessment and weight to be given to the information in the RIRs are the responsibility of independent IRB members (decision-makers) after considering the evidence and arguments presented by the parties.           

The information presented in RIRs solely reflects the views and perspectives of the sources cited and does not necessarily reflect the position of the IRB or the Government of Canada.          

23 September 2026

GIN202692.E

Guinea: Situation and treatment of single women and of women who head their own household, including their ability to live on their own and access housing, income, education, health care, and support services, particularly in Conakry, Kankan, and Nzérékoré; state protection (2024–August 2026)

Research Directorate, Immigration and Refugee Board of Canada

1. Situation and Treatment of Single Women and Women Who Head Their Own Household

In their 2023 thesis for their master's degree in law at the University of Moncton, Souleymane Fofana explains that multiple legal systems coexist [translation] "unofficially" in Guinea, including the State's legal system and "traditional customary law" (2023-01, iii). According to 2023 data compiled by the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD), Guinea's legal framework is "fairly restrictive, limiting gender equality" in matters of divorce and workplace rights (2025-10-06, 3). The US Department of State's Country Reports on Human Rights Practices for 2023 notes that women do not have the "same legal status and rights" as men in "property, employment, credit, and divorce" (US 2024-04-22, 26). The OECD states that articles 136 and 231.5 of Guinea's 2014 Labour Code "restrict" women from working during "night hours" and entering "certain professions that may compromise" their "reproductive capacities," respectively (2025-10-06, 13). US Country Reports 2023 indicates that Guinea's Labour Code "prohibited night work" for women in "factories, mines and quarries, construction sites, shops, and their outbuildings of any kind" (US 2024-04-22, 26). The same source notes that divorce laws "generally favored men in awarding custody and dividing communal assets" (US 2024-04-22, 26).

Freedom House notes that women experience "gender-based disadvantages in laws and practices governing inheritance and property rights" (2024-02-29, Sec. G2).

1.1. Treatment of Single Women, Particularly Widows and Single Mothers

Sources indicate that under Guinean legislation, women have equal rights to inheritance as compared to men (AFJ-G 2026-08-12; ASF France 2022, 15). In correspondence with the Research Directorate, speaking on their own behalf, a communications officer at Amazones de la presse guinéenne, an NGO of women journalists advocating for women's rights in Guinea (FON n.d.a), stated that while by law widows are entitled to a [translation] "share" of their late husband's estate, there is a "legal vacuum" when it comes to customary marriages that were not registered with authorities, making the recognition of inheritance rights "particularly difficult, especially for women in rural areas" (Communications Officer 2026-08-20).

In correspondence with the Research Directorate, the Association of Women Journalists of Guinea (Association des femmes journalistes de Guinée, AFJ-G) stated that widows [translation] "often" experience "rejection, isolation, and sometimes eviction from the marital home by their in-laws" after their husband's death, as well as the "dispossession of their property and land" (2026-08-12). The same source added that widows also face [translation] "intense pressure to remarry," for example to their late husband's brother, "sometimes even against their will" (AFJ-G 2026-08-12).

According to the OECD, Guinea's legal framework establishes "equal rights" between men and women on matters of "land, property, and non-land assets" ownership, with women making up 42% of landowners and house owners (2025-10-06, 3). According to Guinéenews, a Guinea-based digital news portal, widows are [translation] "often deprived of their income and land rights" and find themselves "destitute and unable to feed their children" (2023-11-06).

Guinéenews notes that widows in Guinea face [translation] "extreme poverty, violence, housing deprivation, and legal and social discrimination on a daily basis" (2023-11-06). The AFJ-G stated that although a widow with children [translation] "generally retains a recognized social role" as a mother she remains "economically vulnerable and subject to disinheritance" (2026-08-12). In correspondence with the Research Directorate, Solidarité Suisse-Guinée (SSG) [1] reported, based on interviews with 5 sources [translation] "familiar with the Guinea social context," that widows face challenges after the death of their spouse, including loss of income, childcare responsibilities, "family pressure" related to remarrying, "disputes" regarding inheritance, and "sometimes harassment or rejection" (SSG 2026-08-10). The same source added that these challenges [translation] "do not affect all widows," but are "real risks in certain" social and cultural settings (SSG 2026-08-10). In correspondence with the Research Directorate, the Coalition of Women Leaders of Guinea (Coalition des femmes leaders de Guinée, COFEL), a Conakry-based NGO that seeks to empower women (COFEL n.d.), indicated that following the death of their spouse, widows [translation] "may face" loss of income, "difficulties" with housing and property, "dependence" on their in-laws, and "sometimes pressure to accept certain family decisions" (COFEL 2026-08-12).

SSG indicated that single women, including single mothers and widows, [translation] "can be treated differently in certain social contexts" and that treatment can depend on their "family background, income level, place of residence, cultural and religious norms," as well as their "marital and family status" (2026-08-10). In correspondence with the Research Directorate, Femmes, droit et développement en Afrique Guinée (WILDAF/FEDDAF Guinée), the Guinean chapter of Women in Law and Development in Africa – West Africa (WILDAF-AO), a Togo-based pan-African [translation] "network" of NGOs promoting women's rights (WILDAF-AO n.d.), stated that a single or widowed woman who has no resources, who has dependent children, who has no family support or is [translation] "facing a family conflict, loss of housing or gender-based violence may find herself in a situation of significant vulnerability" (WILDAF-AO 2026-08-25).

According to COFEL, having children [translation] "generally increases vulnerability," as single mothers or widows "must" assume the "entire cost of food, housing, healthcare, and education" for their children (2026-08-12). The same source stated that the situation is [translation] "particularly difficult for women who do not have a family or social network to rely on" (COFEL 2026-08-12). An article published in January 2023 by Guineematin.com, a Guinea-based online news publication, indicates that [translation] "many" single mothers, including those who are divorced or widowed, experience "difficulties of various kinds," namely around "food, clothing, and education needs," and "peaceful coexistence with the community" (2023-01-25). Two single mothers interviewed for an October 2023 article by Guinéenews reported that they have resorted to sex work to support their children; one noted that she tried and failed for [translation] "'years'" to find other employment (2023-10-24).

The AFJ-G reported that [translation] "some" women who have a child out of wedlock are "kicked out from their homes, treated very negatively, and their children themselves may be rejected," adding that having a child out of wedlock is perceived as a "source of family shame" (2026-08-12). SSG noted that [translation] "[i]n some contexts, social attitudes" can "vary" depending on the "circumstances of the children's birth" and the father's level of involvement in the children's lives (2026-08-10). According to COFEL, single mothers can face [translation] "stigma" when having children out of wedlock (2026-08-12). For further information on the situation and treatment of children born out of wedlock in Guinea, see Response to Information Request GIN202694 of September 2026.

Guineematin.com notes that [translation] "some" single, divorced, or widowed mothers encounter "difficulty" in "remarrying" because of "widespread prejudice" (2023-01-25). Citing a 2022 study on gender equality in Guinea published by Gaïa développement, a French consultancy that works in the international cooperation sector (Gaïa développement n.d.), the EU Agency for Asylum (EUAA) reports that divorced or widowed women "often faced challenges in remarrying due to limited suitors, especially if they lacked financial stability, were older, or had children to support" (EU 2024-06-24). In correspondence with the Research Directorate, the Conakry-based NGO Actions for the Health of Mothers and Children in Guinea (Actions pour la santé des mères et des enfants de Guinée, ASMEG) indicated that Guinea's Civil Code (Code civil guinéen) [translation] "discriminat[es]" against women by mandating a 130-day delay before they can remarry [see section 2 of this Response], while men do not have to wait (ASMEG 2026-08-20).

[translation] "Some" sources consulted by SSG reported "social pressure" on women "who have never married, particularly as they grow older" while "othe[r]" sources interviewed by SSG stated that "many" single women live with their families "without being systematically marginalized" (2026-08-10). According to the AFJ-G, single women without children are [translation] "often given little consideration and may sometimes be suspected of having a 'bad character' or of failing to conform to social expectations" (2026-08-12). The same source noted that [translation] "living alone outside the family or marital home" is viewed as "dangerous" and "is frowned upon socially " (AFJ-G 2026-08-12).

2. Legislation

Guinea's Civil Code, adopted in 2019, provides the following:

[translation]

Article 312: A spouse may apply for a divorce on the ground of a prolonged breakdown of cohabitation, if the spouses have lived apart for at least 6 years.

Article 313: A spouse may also apply for a divorce on the ground of a prolonged breakdown of cohabitation, if the other spouse's mental faculties, for at least 6 years, have been so severely impaired that the spouses no longer share spousal cohabitation and there is no reasonable expectation that it will be re-established.

The judge may, on the judge's own motion, reject this application if the divorce could have unduly harsh consequences for the spouse’s health.

…

Article 336: To enter into a new marriage, the wife must observe a waiting period of 130 days.

If the spouses were permitted to live separately during the proceedings, this waiting period starts on the date of the decision allowing separate residences or approving, in the case of a joint application, the temporary agreement granted in this regard.

The wife may remarry immediately if the divorce was granted in accordance with the provisions of articles 312 and 313 of this code.

…

Article 695: If the decedent leaves children or the descendants of such children, the surviving spouse is entitled to one eighth of the estate.

If there are multiple widows, they are entitled to one eighth of the estate.

The share of a childless widow is the same as that of a widow with children.

The widows have a lifetime right to use and enjoy the apartment or any other living space granted to them by their spouse during his lifetime, though the dwelling may not be transferred in a manner that fraudulently deprives the other heirs of their rights.

Article 696: If the decedent has no descendants but leaves one or more ascendants and siblings, the surviving spouse is entitled to one half of the estate.

Article 697: In the absence of descendants and relatives entitled to inherit, the estate passes in its entirety to the surviving spouse. (Guinea 2019, bold in original)

According to a report by the human rights organization Avocats sans frontières France (ASF France) in collaboration with ND Consultance [2], the Civil Code [translation] "legally removes" discrimination against women in inheritance (2022, 15). The same source adds, however, that the makeup of the family council (conseil de famille) [3] and the decisions it makes regarding inheritance "can still be discriminatory" due to "the weight of the stereotypes and roles traditionally assigned to women in Guinean society" (ASF France with ND Consultance 2022, 15).

Citing a [translation] "sociologist and professor at a local private university," Guinéenews reports the following:

"Often, when the husband dies in the hospital, before the body is brought home or to the cemetery, [his] family is already at his home collecting all the documents and belongings before closing up the house. The widow has to go elsewhere with her children, even though in the Family Code, they are the rightful heirs." (2023-11-06)

Guineematin.com notes, citing a lawyer, that while contrary to the law, [translation] "[m]ore often than not… in matters of inheritance, especially when the husband dies," the husband's brothers "evic[t] the widow and disinheri[t] the children" (2026-01-16).

The Labour Code of Guinea provides the following:

[translation]

Article 136.1: Women are prohibited from working at night in factories, manufacturing plants, mines and quarries, construction sites, workshops, and any related facilities of any kind.

The prohibition referred to in the preceding paragraph does not apply to women who hold managerial or technical positions involving responsibility.

Article 136.2: This prohibition does not apply:

-In cases of force majeure, when it is necessary to prevent an interruption in operations;

-When it is necessary to save perishable goods from inevitable loss;

-In cases of successive shifts, if the applicable collective agreement provides for it. In the event of special circumstances requiring women to work at night, the Labour Inspector, upon referral by the employer, may authorize exemptions from the prohibition set forth in paragraph 1 of Article 136.1.

…

Article 231.5: Subject to the provisions of articles 153.1 to 153.6 [4] of this Code, Ministerial Orders prescribe the types of work prohibited for women and pregnant women, or the special protective measures to which they must be subject when performing such work.

Work that is likely to impair a woman's reproductive capacity or, in the case of a pregnant woman, work that affects her health or that of her child is prohibited or shall be subject to special protective measures. (Guinea 2014, bold in original)

3. Access to Housing, Income, Education, Healthcare, and Support Services

3.1. Conakry

The January 2023 article by Guineematin.com notes that [translation] "particularly" in Conakry, it is "not uncommon" to see young women "without a partner or husband" living alone with children, adding that they are "sometimes stigmatized [and] exposed to poverty" (2023-01-25). The AFJ-G stated that [translation] "[i]n general," Conakry offers "more" employment, education and healthcare-related "opportunities" than other cities, which single women might be able to access "without a family network," depending on their "own resources and social capital" (2026-08-12). WILDAF Guinée indicated that an unmarried or widowed woman with [translation] "financial resources, employment, independent housing and a social support network can generally live independently, particularly in Conakry" (WILDAF-AO 2026-08-25).

3.1.1. Access to Housing

The information in the following paragraph was provided by SSG:

Access to housing for single women in Conakry is [translation] "one of the most common challenges" reported by SSG's sources. "Many" of the interviewees reported that "some landlords hesitate or refuse to rent to a single woman or impose specific conditions." Challenges to housing also include "the cost of rent, the guarantees required and the strength of family or social networks." The "barriers" are "greater" for a single woman with children given the need to find housing that is affordable, and is located close enough to work, schools, or essential services, while being responsible for other household expenses (2026-08-10).

The Communications Officer indicated that accessing housing in Conakry can be [translation] "difficult," noting the "high" costs of rent and the "refus[al]" by "some landlords" to rent to single women (2026-08-20). ASMEG stated that access to housing is a [translation] "general problem in urban areas, particularly in Conakry," adding that "economic insecurity" is the "main barrier" to obtaining "decent housing" (2026-08-20). The same source noted that difficulties accessing housing are greater for single women lacking a guarantor, such as a husband or a family (ASMEG 2026-08-20). WILDAF Guinée stated that in Conakry the cost of living, [translation] "particularly housing," is "high," adding that a woman "without a stable income may therefore have difficulty maintaining her independence" (WILDAF-AO 2026-08-25). In correspondence with the Research Directorate, the Maison de la démocratie et des droits de l'homme (M2DH), a Conakry-based NGO that promotes women's rights (M2DH n.d.), stated that [translation] "housing costs, irregular income, landlords' financial requirements, and insufficient income can be significant obstacles" to accessing housing (2026-08-26). The same source indicated that [translation] "discrimination based on marital status" for both men and women is "common" among landlords, but single women have "much greater" difficulty securing housing (M2DH 2026-08-26).

3.1.2. Access to Income

WILDAF Guinée indicated that Conakry offers [translation] "the greatest number" of businesses, services and opportunities for formal employment (WILDAF-AO 2026-08-25). SSG indicated that employment and revenue-generating opportunities [translation] "exist" in "commerce, services, self-employment, non-profit organizations, private companies and public administration"; however, access to "stable" employment "heavily depends" on a person's "education level, professional skills, network, and economic circumstances" (2026-08-10). According to the Communications Officer, finding [translation] "stable employment" is "difficult" for single women in Conakry (2026-08-20). M2DH indicated that [translation] "in practice, access to a stable income" in Conakry is "highly dependent on educational level, professional qualifications, available capital, social network, and employment opportunities" (2026-08-26). The same source added that while the situation is "difficult for everyone," it is "even more complicated" for women in general and "in particular" for single women who "lac[k] strong social ties" in Conakry and who have "multiple intersectional vulnerabilities" (M2DH 2026-08-26).

3.1.3. Access to Education

SSG stated that accessing education and training in Conakry is [translation] "possible" and "no restrictions related to marital status have been reported," adding that single women face difficulties that are "primarily economic and practical," including training and transportation costs, distance from schools, lack of time, lack of childcare, and lack of family support (2026-08-10). M2DH indicated that single women with [translation] "dependent children, low income, [and] limited social capital may face greater difficulties" in accessing education (2026-08-26). The Communications Officer stated that access to education for single women [translation] "primarily" depends on one's "financial means, as it does for the general population" (2026-08-20).

3.1.4. Access to Healthcare

Sources stated that while single women have access to healthcare, in practice, the cost of medical care and transportation can make access to care [translation] "difficult" (SSG 2026-08-10) or [translation] "limited" (M2DH 2026-08-26). M2DH added that in [translation] "some cases," social norms or judgments about their marital status can also limit their access to healthcare (2026-08-26). The same source noted that single women may face [translation] "stigmatizing attitudes" when accessing sexual and reproductive healthcare services (M2DH 2026-08-26). According to the Communications Officer, given that Guinea's healthcare system is [translation] "mostly fee-based," access to healthcare for the population depends "primarily" on "financial means" and is "not directly related to marital status" (2026-08-20). WILDAF Guinée stated that Guinea's Constitution [translation] "provides for the principle of universal health coverage," noting, however, that "effective access to care remains strongly linked to financial resources, the availability of services, and place of residence" (WILDAF-AO 2026-08-25). ASMEG reported that Conakry has [translation] "better access to health infrastructure" than Kankan or Nzérékoré but that healthcare costs nevertheless "remai[n] a major obstacle" and that single mothers "frequently forgo" healthcare because they "often" live in "precarious" circumstances (2026-08-20). WILDAF Guinée stated that access to healthcare for women is [translation] "generally better" in Conakry owing to the "concentration of healthcare infrastructure and specialists" in the city (WILDAF-AO 2026-08-25).

3.1.5. Access to Support Services

According to WILDAF Guinée, Conakry has more women's and human rights organizations than Kankan or Nzérékoré (WILDAF-AO 2026-08-25). Information on state-run support services could not be found among the sources consulted by the Research Directorate within the time constraints of this Response.

3.2. Kankan and Nzérékoré

Information on access to income, education, healthcare, and social services in Kankan and Nzérékoré was scarce among the sources consulted by the Research Directorate within the time constraints of this Response.

3.2.1. Access to Housing

Information on access to housing in Kankan and Nzérékoré could not be found among the sources consulted by the Research Directorate within the time constraints of this Response.

3.2.2. Access to Income

WILDAF Guinée indicated that [translation] "formal" employment opportunities for people living in Kankan are "more limited than in Conakry," and that women are "more dependent on commercial, agricultural, artisanal, or informal activities" (WILDAF-AO 2026-08-25). The same source noted that while work in the trade, agriculture, services, and development sectors is available in Nzérékoré, [translation] "formal" employment opportunities are "more limited" than in Conakry (WILDAF-AO 2026-08-25). Further and corroborating information could not be found among the sources consulted by the Research Directorate within the time constraints of this Response.

3.2.3. Access to Education, Healthcare, and Social Services

According to ASMEG, women from Kankan or Nzérékoré, [translation] "especially" those in "rural areas," are "far more disadvantaged" than women in Conakry, adding that "disparities in access to services," such as healthcare and education, are "significant" between Conakry and Guinea's "interior regions" (2026-08-20). WILDAF Guinée stated that healthcare services are available in Kankan and Nzérékoré, but the [translation] "specialized" services are "more limited" and women "may have to travel for certain treatments" (WILDAF-AO 2026-08-25). COFEL noted that the [translation] "availability" and "quality" of healthcare services are "more limited outside of Conakry" (2026-08-12).

According to WILDAF Guinée, in Nzérékoré in 2025, regional authorities reportedly provided psychosocial and legal support to a number of survivors of gender-based violence (WILDAF-AO 2026-08-25). Further and corroborating information on single women's access to social services in Kankan and Nzérékoré could not be found among the sources consulted by the Research Directorate within the time constraints of this Response.

4. State Protection

COFEL stated that the state has [translation] "protection mechanisms and several programs" for women, adding, however that the mechanisms do not represent a "sufficiently broad social protection" to enable a single or widowed woman "without resources" to live "independently" (2026-08-12). The same source noted that there is [translation] "no national mechanism that automatically guarantees" women a "minimum income," housing, "regular" child support, "full" healthcare coverage, or "specific protection" based "solely" on her marital status (COFEL 2026-08-12). According to sources, there are no [translation] "specific measures" dedicated to single mothers (M2DH 2026-08-26; AFJ-G 2026-08-12). Sources also noted that state protection mechanisms target [translation] "general economic vulnerability (AFJ-G 2026-08-12) or [translation] "vulnerable populations as a whole" (M2DH 2026-08-26). Citing the sources it interviewed, SSG noted that there are public sector, non-government and community organizations that support women and vulnerable people, but there are no resources nationwide to specifically support single women or widows (2026-08-10).

Sources describe the following state protection mechanisms for women:

  • The policies of the Ministry of Women, Family and Solidarity (ministère de la Femme, de la Famille et des Solidarités) aimed at women's protection [5] (ASMEG 2026-08-20; AFJ-G 2026-08-12).
  • Fund to Support the Economic Activities of Women and Girls (Fonds d'appui aux activités économiques des femmes et des filles) (ASMEG 2026-08-20). The fund is a [translation] "public administrative institution" with a mission to finance and support "economic projects and programs" benefiting women and girls in Guinea (Guinea n.d.b).
  • Women and Girls' Empowerment and Entrepreneurship Centre (Centre d'autonomisation et d'entrepreneuriat des femmes/filles) [6] (SSG 2026-08-10; Guinea n.d.c). There are 35 such centres nationwide that provide [translation] "solutions" for "economic and social empowerment" to "vulnerable women" and those who "never attended or did not complete school" (Guinea n.d.c).
  • Social Development and Extreme Poverty Fund (Fonds de développement social et de l'indigence, FDSI) (AFJ-G 2026-08-12; Africaguinee.com 2022-08-05). The FDSI is a government agency with a mission to implement [translation] "socio-economic integration programs and social safety nets, as well as to improve access to healthcare" for people living in extreme poverty (Guinea n.d.d).

According to M2DH, the FDSI [translation] "target[s]… widows" among other groups in its "socioeconomic integration programs" (2026-08-26). Africaguinee.com, a digital news publication, states that in August 2022 the FDSI deployed across 5 communes in Conakry to identify people living in extreme poverty who will be able to benefit from free healthcare assistance, including a widow who also received 5 bags of rice from the agency (Africaguinee.com 2022-08-05). Guinee7.com, a Guinea-based digital news outlet, reports that in November 2022, the Directors General of the FDSI and the Labé Regional Hospital signed a memorandum of understanding under which 27 patients, including the minor child of a widow, would receive [translation] "fully covered" medical treatment (Guinee7.com 2026-08-10).

The National Agency for Economic and Social Inclusion (Agence nationale d'inclusion économique et sociale, ANIES), a government agency with a mission to contribute to [translation] "poverty reduction" and "a better distribution of economic prosperity" in Guinea (Guinea n.d.e), reports that through its Emergency Response and Nafa Program Support Project (Projet de réponse d'urgence et d'appui au Programme Nafa, PRU-APN), the national government [translation] has supported 136,000 households, "many" of whom are widows, by "ensur[ing] their basic needs are met" (Guinea 2024-03-13). According to Guinéenews, through Nafa, the government has provided [translation] "emergency cash transfers" to "over" 130,000 households in response to the "socioeconomic impacts of the COVID-19 pandemic and other shocks" (2025-08-07). The World Bank reports, based on a mission conducted in June 2026, that [translation] "a significant number" of beneficiaries are unable to withdraw their funds "within the scheduled timeframe" owing to "a shortage of funds at service points and among distribution agents," adding that challenges stem from "liquidity constraints" affecting Guinea and "the network of agents responsible for withdrawal operations" (World Bank 2026-08-30, 1, 5). The same source notes that the PRU-APN is set to end in December 2026 (World Bank 2026-08-30, 2).

ASMEG indicated that government measures to support women are [translation] "significant on paper," adding, however, that they are of "limited and uneven effectiveness" (2026-08-20). COFEL stated that existing measures face problems with [translation] "geographical coverage, financial resources, staffing, beneficiary awareness and accessibility" (2026-08-12). According to the same source, the existence of support mechanisms [translation] "does not necessarily mean that vulnerable women can effectively access them" (COFEL 2026-08-12). The Communications Officer indicated that [translation] "in practice," state protection mechanisms are "limited" and "their effectiveness may be insufficient" (2026-08-20). SSG noted that [translation] "some" sources it consulted considered publicly funded support measures "relatively effective," while others indicated that "some" programs are "insufficiently functional, poorly adapted to the needs of women concerned, or difficult to access" owing to factors such as "lack of information, distance, cost, administrative procedures," or the limited number of entities providing services (2026-08-10). M2DH noted that state protection mechanisms face [translation] "[s]everal challenges," including:

  • insufficient information for beneficiaries about available mechanisms
  • geographical disparities in access to services
  • economic difficulties faced by beneficiaries
  • costs related to [specific] procedures and [access to] justice
  • the slowness of certain processes,
  • the persistence of social norms unfavourable to women, and
  • insufficient specific data to measure the situation of single women (2026-
  • 08-26).

This Response was prepared after researching publicly accessible information currently available to the Research Directorate within time constraints. This Response is not, and does not purport to be, conclusive as to the merit of any particular claim for refugee protection.
