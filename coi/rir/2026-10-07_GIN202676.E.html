Responses to Information Requests

Page Content

​​​​​​​Responses to Information Requests (RIRs) are research reports on country conditions. They are requested by IRB decision-makers.

The database contains a seven-year archive of English and French RIRs. Earlier RIRs may be found on the European Country of Origin Information Network website.

RIR​s published by the IRB on its website may have attachments that are inaccessible due to technical constraints and may include translations of documents originally written in languages other than English or French. To obtain a copy of such attachments and/or translated version of the RIR attachments, please email us.​

Disclaimer

Responses to Information Requests (RIRs) cite publicly accessible information available at the time of publication and within time constraints. A list of references and additional sources consulted are included in each RIR. Sources cited are considered the most current information available as of the date of the RIR.            

RIRs are not, and do not purport to be, conclusive as to the merit of any particular claim for refugee protection. Rather, they are intended to support the refugee determination process. More information on the methodology used by the Research Directorate can be found here.          

The assessment and weight to be given to the information in the RIRs are the responsibility of independent IRB members (decision-makers) after considering the evidence and arguments presented by the parties.           

The information presented in RIRs solely reflects the views and perspectives of the sources cited and does not necessarily reflect the position of the IRB or the Government of Canada.          

7 October 2026

GIN202676.E

Guinea: Domestic violence and the treatment of survivors by society and authorities; access to support services and state protection (2024–September 2026)

Research Directorate, Immigration and Refugee Board of Canada

1. Overview

The UNDP Gender Inequality Index of 2023, which measures gender inequality across 3 dimensions: reproductive health, empowerment, and the labour market, ranks Guinea 163rd of 172 countries (UN 2026-05-06). In its 2026 annual report to the UN on its implementation of sustainable development goals, Guinea's Ministère du Plan, de la Coopération internationale et du Développement (MPCID) lists female genital mutilation (FGM), child marriage, and gender-based violence (GBV) among the factors that make Guinea one of "the countries most affected by gender inequalities" (Guinea 2026-08-10, 156).

According to an interview with the Divisional Commissioner and Director General of Guinea's Office for Gender Protection, Childhood, and Morals (Office de Protection du genre, de l'enfance, et des mœurs, OPROGEM) by the news website Guineematin.com, GBV [translation] "particularly rape," "remains prevalent" (2023-12-30). Similarly, the UN Committee on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights describes the number of incidents of violence against women, especially domestic and sexual violence, as "very high" in the country, as cited in a report for the UN Universal Periodic Review (UPR) for Guinea (UN 2025-02-04, para. 45).

A report on the status of women and girls in Guinea by staff of the World Bank and other contributors notes that there is limited data available on GBV in the country and a "lack of information on women's rights" (de Paz, et al. 2023-05-10, 79). In the UN's UPR for Guinea, the UN country team notes the absence of a "functioning national mechanism for collecting data" on GBV and challenges to raising awareness on issues of violence against women (UN 2025-02-04, para. 46). According to an interview conducted by the Network of Women Leaders for Development (Réseau des femmes leaders pour le développement, RFLD) [1] with a state official [translation] "engaged in the fight against violence in Guinea," cases of domestic violence are underreported due to stigmatization, fear of repercussions, and a lack of public awareness of such issues (RFLD 2025-01-10).

Based on an article on the Guinean online news website Conakryinfos.com, a report by the Advocates for Human Rights (The Advocates), an NGO that provides legal services to asylum claimants in the US, and Timothy D. Sanders Szabo, an attorney at Stinson LLP who specializes in immigration law, Guinea's National Directorate for the Promotion of Women and Gender puts the percentage of married Guinean women who have experienced emotional violence at 87.7%, psychological violence at 75.7%, and sexual violence at 49.6% (The Advocates & Sanders Szabo 2024-10-11, 8).

1.1. Legislation

According to the report published by the World Bank, Guinea's parallel legal system of customary/religious laws and statutory laws leads to "confusion and often undermines women's rights," as the former set of laws "often supersede" the latter (de Paz, et al. 2023-05-10, 79). For instance, the source indicates that cases of GBV and access to justice for women "may often" fall under the customary legal and justice system rather than under the statutory one, resulting in "impunity of perpetrators" (de Paz, et al. 2023-05-10, 85).

The report by the Advocates and Sanders Szabo notes that although the criminal code punishes GBV, including rape, sexual assault, battery, assault, and spousal violence, "authorities do not consistently enforce the law" (2024-10-11, 1, 7).

According to the Guinean state official interviewed by RFLD, there are no specific laws geared towards protecting victims of domestic violence in Guinea (2025-01-10). The Advocates and Sanders Szabo indicate that existing legal provisions do not cover "low-level injuries, threats of bodily harm, and the use of power and control in the context of domestic violence" (2024-10-11, 7). The report published by the World Bank states that although "[s]ome forms" of GBV may be prosecuted under the criminal code if they fall under the charges of murder, rape, indecent assault, or assault and battery, there is "no standalone law that criminalizes domestic abuse or sexual harassment" (de Paz, et al. 2023-05-10, 77). For instance, Guinea's national report to the UN's UPR notes that marital rape is not codified in its criminal code but adds that "as the current definition of rape makes no distinction regarding the perpetrator of the offence, there are no obstacles to the punishment of rape in the marital context" (Guinea 2025-02-17, para. 115).

The report published by the World Bank also indicates the following regarding domestic violence legislation in Guinea:

  • Guinean law does not require authorities to issue protective orders for reporting survivors; and
  • Guinea has yet to introduce a definition of domestic violence that comprises physical, psychological, sexual, and economic types of violence (de Paz, et al. 2023-05-10, 77).

Guinea's 2016 Penal Code (Loi No 2016/059/AN portant Code pénal) provides the following:

[translation]

SECTION II: ASSAULT, INJURY AND WILFUL VIOLENCE

Article 239: Any person who wilfully inflicts blows or causes injuries resulting in an inability to work for less than 20 days shall be punished by imprisonment for 16 days to 2 years and a fine of 500,000 to 1,000,000 Guinean francs [C$78 to C$156], or by one of these two penalties alone.

In the case of premeditation or ambush, the imprisonment shall be from 2 to 5 years and the fine from 1,000,000 to 2,000,000 Guinean francs, or either of these two penalties alone.

Article 240: Any person who wilfully commits acts of violence or assault likely to cause serious distress to a reasonable person shall be punished by imprisonment for a term of 16 days to 1 year and a fine of 500,000 to 1,000,000 Guinean francs, or by one of these two penalties alone.

…

SECTION V: SEXUAL ASSAULT AND INDECENT ASSAULT

Article 267: A sexual assault is any sexual act committed through violence, coercion, threats or surprise.

PARAGRAPH I: RAPE

Article 268: Rape is any act of sexual penetration, of any nature whatsoever, committed against another person using violence, coercion or surprise.

Rape is punishable by imprisonment for 5 to 10 years.

Rape is punishable by imprisonment for 10 to 20 years if:

1. it results in mutilation or permanent disability;

2. it is committed against a minor under the age of 18;

3. it is committed against a person whose particular vulnerability is due to age, illness, disability, physical or mental impairment, or a state of pregnancy that is apparent or known to the perpetrator;

4. it is committed by a direct ascendant or any other person exercising legal or de facto authority over the victim;

5. it is committed by a person who abuses the authority conferred by their office;

6. it is committed by multiple persons acting as perpetrators or accomplices;

7. it is committed with the use or threat of a weapon;

8. the victim was brought into contact with the perpetrator through the use of an electronic communications network to disseminate messages to an unspecified audience;

9. it is committed in conjunction with one or more other rapes;

10. it is committed by a person acting in a state of obvious intoxication or under the obvious influence of narcotics;

11. it is committed following the administration of substances likely to impair the victim's consent.

Article 269: Rape is punishable by life imprisonment if:

1. it is preceded, accompanied or followed by torture or acts of barbarity;

2. it results in the death of the victim.

Article 270: Attempted rape is punishable in the same way as rape itself.

…

PARAGRAPH IV: SEXUAL HARASSMENT

Article 277: Sexual harassment is the act of repeatedly subjecting a person to remarks or behaviour of a sexual nature that either violate that person's dignity because of their degrading or humiliating nature, or create an intimidating, hostile or offensive situation for that person.

Sexual harassment includes any act—even a single occurrence—in which serious pressure is exerted with the actual or apparent intent of obtaining a sexual act, whether such an act is sought for the benefit of the perpetrator or for the benefit of a third party.

The acts referred to in paragraphs 1 and 2 are punishable by imprisonment for a term of 3 months to 1 year and a fine of 500,000 to 1,000,000 Guinean francs, or by either of these two penalties alone.

These penalties are increased to two years' imprisonment and a fine of 2,000,000 Guinean francs when the acts are committed:

1. by a person who abuses the authority conferred by their office;

2. against a minor under the age of 18;

3. against a person whose particular vulnerability is due to their age, illness, disability, physical or mental impairment, or a state of pregnancy that is apparent or known to the perpetrator;

4. against a person whose particular vulnerability or dependence, resulting from the precarious economic or social situation, is apparent or known to the perpetrator;

5. by multiple persons acting as perpetrators or accomplices. (Guinea 2016, bold in original)

2. Treatment of Survivors of Domestic Violence

2.1. Treatment by Society

Sources indicate that societal pressure placed on survivors of GBV has led to an underreporting of cases in Guinea (Monénembo 2023-01-08; Terre des hommes 2026-02-05). Similarly, the Guinean state official interviewed by RFLD reported that while the pressure of customs and norms [translation] "that assign women a subordinate and inferior role to men, and that justify violence as a means of control and discipline," are factors that contribute to the high rate of violence against women, he added that factors like poverty and challenges to accessing education, employment, health, and justice also increase women's vulnerability and dependency on their spouses (2025-01-10).

According to the report published by the World Bank, Guinean society has a "[h]igh acceptance" of GBV, and survivors who seek support or report an incident face "strong social stigma" that "discourage women from accessing justice" (de Paz, et al. 2023-05-10, 77). The source adds that social and cultural norms dictate that GBV is a "family matter," and that submitting a complaint against one's husband is unacceptable (de Paz, et al. 2023-05-10, 77). According to a survey results published in 2024 on the perception of GBV in Guinea, conducted by Afrobarometer in partnership with Stat View International (SVI) [2], which surveyed 1,200 adults in August 2022, 76% of respondents saw intimate partner violence as a [translation] "private matter to be resolved within the family rather than a criminal matter" (Afrobarometer 2024-03-08, 2). Additional survey findings include the following:

  • 67% believe that it is either [translation] "sometimes" or "always" "justified for a man to beat his wife";
  • 76% believe it is either "somewhat likely" or "very likely" that "police take complaints of GBV seriously"; although 60% say that it is either "somewhat likely" or "very likely" that survivors of GBV who report it to the police "face criticism, harassment or humiliation from members of their community";
  • 53% are disapproving of the government's handling of women's rights; and
  • 72% demand "greater efforts" in this regard (Afrobarometer 2024-03-08, 2, 4, 7).

In an interview with the Advocates and Sanders Szabo, an "individual with lived experience expertise" in GBV in Guinea indicated that cases of GBV "are prevalent in rural areas" (2024-10-11, 8). The interviewee added that survivors of GBV who live in rural areas deal with "fear of criticism and social exclusion," which keep them from coming forward (The Advocates & Szabo 2024-10-11, 8). As a result, GBV cases, including domestic child sexual assault, are "frequently concealed, as mothers fear the stigma associated with disclosing that their daughters have been assaulted" (The Advocates & Sanders Szabo 2024-10-11, 8).

2.2. Treatment by Authorities

Information on the treatment of domestic violence survivors in Guinea by authorities was scarce among the sources consulted by the Research Directorate within the time constraints of this Response.

According to the Advocates and Sanders Szabo, reports of GBV are met by authorities with "inaction and revictimization," and survivors are not provided with protection and relief from perpetrators (2024-10-11, 7, 8). The Guinean state official interviewed by RFLD indicated that legal, police, and medical professionals do not have adequate training and awareness to address issues of domestic violence, resulting in a lack of reporting and support services, as well as punishment of perpetrators (2025-01-10).

An article on the website Guinéenews reports that, in the city of Mamou, cases of domestic violence have become [translation] "very common," and that in the same region, a man's attack on his wife in June 2023 left her hospitalized and with a broken jaw (2023-07-24). Mamou's Prefectural Directorate for the Promotion of Women, Children, and Vulnerable Persons, along with OPROGEM, were contacted as the primary resources for domestic violence cases, and the Prefectural Director explained the organizations' response as follows:

[translation]

"When we are informed, we investigate the facts. We first summon the spouse to discuss the situation. Then, we contact the couple's parents and both spouses to try to reach an amicable agreement. We raise awareness about everyone's responsibilities and have them sign a commitment so that, in the event of a repeat offense, we refer them to a more appropriate service (the gendarmerie or the police) for standard legal proceedings. If there is excessive violence, it can lead to divorce." (Guinéenews 2023-07-24)

3. Support Services

Information on support services available to domestic violence survivors in Guinea was scarce among the sources consulted by the Research Directorate within the time constraints of this Response.

The report published by the World Bank states that survivors of domestic violence are confronted with limited health, psychosocial, and other services, and the services that are available "lack a survivor-centered approach" (de Paz, et al. 2023-05-10, 79). The source adds that cost, "limitations" in quality of services, and intransigent referral processes further hinder survivors' ability to seek help (de Paz, et al. 2023-05-10, 79).

4. State Protection

According to the Advocates and Sanders Szabo, survivors of GBV face "significant barriers" to accessing support services and justice (2024-10-11, 7). The report published by the World Bank states that gaps in Guinean law and policy, "poor enforcement and institutional capacity," and deep-rooted customs and practices together constitute "major obstacles to effective GBV prevention and response in Guinea" (de Paz, et al. 2023-05-10, 77). The same source lists protection services as among those that are limited for survivors of domestic violence (2024-06-12, 79).

A 2019 Guinea government report notes that its national plan for the period of 2016−2020 contains measures to address violence against women, including establishing GBV centres in health facilities, GBV units in police stations, and investigative units within the gendarmerie, creating a gender and equity office in the Ministry of National Defense (ministère de la Défense nationale), launching the OPROGEM [created in 2009 (Monénembo 2023-01-08)] and implementing a training program for defense and security forces (Guinea 2019-05, 2, 6, 8). The same source adds that it raised awareness of violence against girls in the prefectures of Kouroussa and Dinguiraye (Guinea 2019-05, 4). Guinea's national report to the UN's UPR indicates that the government implemented a 2021 protocol addressing GBV and FGM, which led to the establishment of the Service for the Protection of Vulnerable Persons in the High Command of the National gendarmerie, which "strengthens collaboration" between the security forces and the juvenile court (Guinea 2025-02-17, para. 112). The government also created a database in the National Directorate for the Advancement of Women and Gender (Direction nationale de la promotion féminine et du genre, DNPFG), "uniting stakeholders" who report GBV cases (Guinea 2025-02-17, para. 112). However, the same source adds that "[d]espite these measures," GBV "persists" (Guinea 2025-02-17, para. 113). Additionally, in an op-ed in weekly French news magazine Le Point, Guinean author Tierno Monénembo writes that Guinea created the National Observatory for the Fight Against Gender-Based Violence (Observatoire national de la lutte contre les violences basées sur le genre) in 2011 (Monénembo 2023-01-08). He adds, however, that this Observatory and OPROGEM have failed to [translation] "stem the tide of the [GBV] problem," due to the persistent legal vacuum and the challenge of enforcing the law (Monénembo 2023-01-08).

Guineematin.com reports that all 205 cases of rape OPROGEM recorded in 2023 are being handled through the courts, and most of the [translation] "numerous other GBV" incidents registered are also going through the justice system (2023-12-30). In addition, the Director General of OPROGEM announced that the office recorded 95 cases of violence, in the category including domestic, physical, psychological, and physiological, 35 of which are going through the courts (Guineematin.com 2023-12-30). The Director General further stated that when OPROGEM receives a case of intimate partner violence, they refer it to a forensic doctor to obtain [translation] "'diagnostics'" and legal-medical reports in case the court requires it (Guineematin.com 2023-12-30).
